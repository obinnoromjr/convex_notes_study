Quasiconvex functions: basic properties

One inequality replaces the sublevel-set bookkeeping. On a segment, a quasiconvex function never rises above the larger of its two endpoint values — the convex combination of Jensen swapped for a max.

§0where we are

From sublevel sets to a single inequality

§3.4.1 defined quasiconvexity through sublevel sets: \(f\) is quasiconvex when \(\mathrm{dom}\,f\) is convex and every sublevel set \(S_\alpha=\{x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f\mid f(x)\le\alpha\}\) is a convex set. That definition is clean, but it quantifies over all levels \(\alpha\), which is awkward to check by hand. This subsection gives the equivalent Jensen-style characterization — the form you actually use to recognize or prove quasiconvexity — together with the min-form dual for quasiconcave functions, two named examples (cardinality and rank), the fact that quasiconvexity is decided one line at a time, and the complete picture on \(\mathbb{R}\).

§1the characterization

Jensen's inequality for quasiconvex functions

A function \(f\) is quasiconvex if and only if \(\mathrm{dom}\,f\) is convex and, for all \(x,y\in\mathrm{dom}\,f\) and \(0\le\theta\le 1\), \[ f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\;\le\;\max\{f(x),\,f(y)\}. \tag{3.19}\] B&V §3.4.2 · L5056–5064

In words: the value on a segment does not exceed the maximum of the two endpoint values (Figure 3.10). This is the quasiconvex analogue of ordinary Jensen's inequality — with \(\max\{f(x),f(y)\}\) standing in place of the convex combination \(\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)\).

  • weaker
    Since \(\max\{f(x),f(y)\}\ \ge\ \theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)\) for every \(\theta\in[0,1]\), condition (3.19) is implied by convexity. That is exactly why convex \(\Rightarrow\) quasiconvex — and why the converse fails: \(f\) is free to sit anywhere in the gap between the chord and the max.
  • matches
    It agrees with the sublevel-set definition: if both endpoints satisfy \(f\le\alpha\), then (3.19) forces \(f\le\alpha\) on the entire segment, so \(S_\alpha\) is convex.
  • use
    (3.19) is a statement about two points at a time, with no free level parameter \(\alpha\) — which is why it is the practical test.
Claim  (3.19) \(\Longleftrightarrow\) every sublevel set convexboth directions, two lines each
\[ f(x)\le\alpha,\; f(y)\le\alpha \;\Longrightarrow\; f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\max\{f(x),f(y)\}\le\alpha \]
(3.19) ⟹ convex sublevel sets. take any \(x,y\in S_\alpha\); the max of two numbers \(\le\alpha\) is \(\le\alpha\), so the whole segment lands in \(S_\alpha\).

the domain condition is carried along in both statements — without it \(\theta x+(1-\theta)y\) need not be a point where \(f\) is defined.
!

The choice \(\alpha=\max\{f(x),f(y)\}\) is the whole trick. It is the smallest level containing both points, so convexity of that single sublevel set already delivers the inequality for that pair.

§2the dual form

The quasiconcave "modified Jensen" — min in place of max

Recall \(f\) is quasiconcave exactly when \(-f\) is quasiconvex. Applying (3.19) to \(-f\) and flipping signs gives the mirror statement.

\(f\) is quasiconcave if and only if \(\mathrm{dom}\,f\) is convex and, for all \(x,y\in\mathrm{dom}\,f\) and \(0\le\theta\le1\), \[ f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\;\ge\;\min\{f(x),\,f(y)\}. \] B&V §3.4.2 · L5064–5066

The value on a segment is at least the minimum of the endpoint values. This is the form the next two examples use: both Example 3.35 and Example 3.36 are proved by exhibiting a superadditivity-type inequality \(g(x+y)\ge\min\{g(x),g(y)\}\) and reading it as the min-form condition.

!

Three inequalities live in the same picture and are easy to swap. \(\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)\) as an upper bound is convexity. \(\max\{f(x),f(y)\}\) as an upper bound is quasiconvexity. \(\min\{f(x),f(y)\}\) as a lower bound is quasiconcavity. Only the middle one is (3.19).

§3example 3.35

Cardinality of a nonnegative vector

Example 3.35 (Cardinality of a nonnegative vector). The cardinality or size of \(x\in\mathbb{R}^n\) is the number of nonzero components, denoted \(\mathbf{card}(x)\). The function \(\mathbf{card}\) is quasiconcave on \(\mathbb{R}^n_+\) (but not \(\mathbb{R}^n\)) since it satisfies \[ \mathbf{card}(x+y)\;\ge\;\min\{\mathbf{card}(x),\,\mathbf{card}(y)\} \] for \(x,y\succeq 0\). B&V §3.4.2 · L5066–5074

The mechanism is cancellation, or rather its absence. For \(x,y\succeq 0\) the support of the sum contains the union of the supports: a component \((x+y)_i=x_i+y_i\) is a sum of two nonnegative numbers, so it can only vanish if both vanish. Hence

\[ \mathrm{supp}(x+y)=\mathrm{supp}(x)\cup\mathrm{supp}(y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{card}(x+y)\ \ge\ \max\{\mathbf{card}(x),\mathbf{card}(y)\}\ \ge\ \min\{\cdots\}. \]
!

On all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) the claim fails, and the counterexample is as short as possible: take \(y=-x\) with \(x\neq0\). Then \(\mathbf{card}(x+y)=\mathbf{card}(0)=0\) while \(\min\{\mathbf{card}(x),\mathbf{card}(-x)\}=\mathbf{card}(x)>0\). Cancellation destroys the support. The restriction to the nonnegative orthant is not cosmetic.

reading the inequality as the min-form conditionwhy this proves quasiconcavity

For \(x,y\succeq 0\) and \(\theta\in[0,1]\), both \(\theta x\) and \((1-\theta)y\) are again nonnegative, and scaling by a positive number does not change the support: \(\mathbf{card}(\theta x)=\mathbf{card}(x)\) for \(\theta>0\). So

\[ \mathbf{card}(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\ \ge\ \min\{\mathbf{card}(\theta x),\mathbf{card}((1-\theta)y)\}=\min\{\mathbf{card}(x),\mathbf{card}(y)\}, \]

which is exactly the min-form condition of §2 on the convex set \(\mathbb{R}^n_+\). The endpoint cases \(\theta\in\{0,1\}\) are trivial.

§4example 3.36

Rank of a positive semidefinite matrix

Example 3.36 (Rank of positive semidefinite matrix). The function \(\mathbf{rank}\,X\) is quasiconcave on \(\mathbf{S}^n_+\), since \[ \mathbf{rank}(X+Y)\;\ge\;\min\{\mathbf{rank}\,X,\,\mathbf{rank}\,Y\} \] for \(X,Y\in\mathbf{S}^n_+\). This is the matrix analogue of the cardinality example. B&V §3.4.2 · L5077–5084

The analogy is exact, with range in place of support. For PSD matrices \(\mathcal{R}(X+Y)=\mathcal{R}(X)+\mathcal{R}(Y)\supseteq\mathcal{R}(X)\), because \(v^\top(X+Y)v=v^\top Xv+v^\top Yv\) is a sum of nonnegative terms, so the nullspaces intersect: \(\mathcal{N}(X+Y)=\mathcal{N}(X)\cap\mathcal{N}(Y)\). A smaller nullspace means a larger rank, giving \(\mathbf{rank}(X+Y)\ge\max\{\mathbf{rank}\,X,\mathbf{rank}\,Y\}\).

cardinality is the diagonal caserank(diag x) = card(x)

For \(x\succeq0\), the matrix \(\mathbf{diag}(x)\) is positive semidefinite and \(\mathbf{rank}(\mathbf{diag}(x))=\mathbf{card}(x)\): a diagonal matrix has one nonzero eigenvalue per nonzero entry. Restricting the rank inequality to diagonal PSD matrices recovers Example 3.35 exactly, which is why §4 is said to extend §3 rather than merely resemble it.

!

As with cardinality, the PSD hypothesis is doing real work — it is the analogue of \(x\succeq0\). Drop it and cancellation returns: for symmetric but indefinite matrices take \(Y=-X\), so \(\mathbf{rank}(X+Y)=0\) while both ranks are positive.

§5reduction to one dimension

Quasiconvexity on lines

A function is quasiconvex if and only if its restriction to any line intersecting its domain is quasiconvex. B&V §3.4.2 · L5105–5109

Exactly as for convexity, quasiconvexity is a one-dimensional property. This is immediate from (3.19), which only ever involves the two points \(x,y\) and the segment between them — all of which lie on a single line. So to verify quasiconvexity you may restrict \(f\) to an arbitrary line \(\{x_0+tv\mid t\in\mathbb{R}\}\), obtain a function of the single variable \(t\), and check quasiconvexity there.

↓

This is what makes §6 useful rather than a curiosity: once the problem is one-dimensional, the classification of continuous quasiconvex functions on \(\mathbb{R}\) is a complete answer, and the test becomes "is every restriction monotone or valley-shaped?"

§6the picture on R

Continuous quasiconvex functions on \(\mathbb{R}\)

A continuous function \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is quasiconvex if and only if at least one of the following holds:
· \(f\) is nondecreasing;
· \(f\) is nonincreasing;
· there is a point \(c\in\mathrm{dom}\,f\) such that for \(t\le c\), \(f\) is nonincreasing, and for \(t\ge c\), \(f\) is nondecreasing.
The point \(c\) can be taken as any point which is a global minimizer of \(f\). B&V §3.4.2 · L5111–5125

In one picture: monotone, or valley-shaped — down and then up, unimodal, with no strict interior peak between two lower endpoints (Figure 3.11). The two monotone cases are the degenerate valleys with the turning point pushed to an endpoint of the domain.

  • continuity
    The hypothesis matters. Without it the trichotomy fails to be exhaustive — a quasiconvex function on \(\mathbb{R}\) may jump around, as \(\lceil x\rceil\) and the indicator-style examples of §3.4.1 already show, while still having interval sublevel sets.
  • the c
    Any global minimizer serves as \(c\); it need not be unique, and \(f\) may be flat on an interval of minimizers.
  • test
    Combined with §5: to disprove quasiconvexity of a function on \(\mathbb{R}^n\), find one line whose restriction has a strict interior peak.

§7expert lens · diagnose

What to have at your fingertips

01(3.19) is the working definition

Quasiconvex \(\iff\) \(\mathrm{dom}\,f\) convex and \(f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\max\{f(x),f(y)\}\) — value on a segment \(\le\) the larger endpoint. Weaker than convexity, hence convex \(\Rightarrow\) quasiconvex.

02Min-form dual

Quasiconcave \(\iff\) \(f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\ge\min\{f(x),f(y)\}\).

03card and rank

Quasiconcave via \(\mathbf{card}(x+y)\ge\min\{\mathbf{card}\,x,\mathbf{card}\,y\}\) for \(x,y\succeq0\) and \(\mathbf{rank}(X+Y)\ge\min\{\mathbf{rank}\,X,\mathbf{rank}\,Y\}\) for \(X,Y\succeq0\) — only on the nonnegative orthant / PSD cone, since cancellation breaks it. And \(\mathbf{rank}(\mathbf{diag}(x))=\mathbf{card}(x)\).

04Line restriction

Quasiconvex \(\iff\) quasiconvex on every line meeting the domain.

05On \(\mathbb{R}\), continuous

Quasiconvex \(\iff\) nondecreasing, or nonincreasing, or valley-shaped (nonincreasing up to \(c\), nondecreasing after, \(c\) a global minimizer).

06Traps

(3.19) uses \(\max\), not the convex combination (that is convexity) and not \(\min\) (that is quasiconcavity). card and rank are quasiconcave, not convex, and only on \(\mathbb{R}^n_+\)/\(\mathbf{S}^n_+\). "Valley" permits flat stretches but no strict interior peak.
