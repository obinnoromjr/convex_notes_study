The conjugate function: basic properties

Five facts you actually use. One inequality that falls straight out of the definition, one closedness condition that decides whether \(f^{**}=f\), one gradient identity for the smooth case, and three exact calculus rules — each with a trap attached.

§0where we are

§3.3.1 defined the conjugate \(f^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\operatorname{dom}f}\bigl(y^{\mathsf T}x-f(x)\bigr)\) and computed examples. This subsection collects the five basic properties you actually use: Fenchel's inequality, the biconjugate identity \(f^{**}=f\), the Legendre form for differentiable \(f\), the scaling / affine-composition rules, and the conjugate of a sum of independent functions.

§1Fenchel's inequality

A sup dominates each of its members

The first property is not so much proved as observed. The conjugate is a supremum over \(x\); a supremum is at least as large as any single member of the set it is taken over. So for every \(x\),

\(f^{*}(y)\ \ge\ y^{\mathsf T}x-f(x)\) for every \(x\), and rearranged, \(f(x)+f^{*}(y)\ \ge\ x^{\mathsf T}y\) for all \(x,y\) — Fenchel's inequality (called Young's inequality when \(f\) is differentiable). B&V §3.3.2 · L4853–4859
Claim — \(f(x)+f^{*}(y)\ge x^{\mathsf T}y\) for all \(x,y\)one line from the definition
\[ f^{*}(y)=\sup_{x'\in\operatorname{dom}f}\bigl(y^{\mathsf T}x'-f(x')\bigr)\ \ge\ y^{\mathsf T}x-f(x) \]
why a supremum dominates each member of the set. Take \(x'=x\); no optimality, no convexity, no differentiability is used.
\[ f(x)+f^{*}(y)\ \ge\ x^{\mathsf T}y \]
why add \(f(x)\) to both sides. That is the whole proof — it is the definition rearranged.

Trivial as the derivation is, this inequality is the workhorse of conjugate duality. It is the one relation linking a point \(x\) in the primal space to a point \(y\) in the dual space, valid without any hypothesis on \(f\) at all.

Instance — the quadratic pair\(f=\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Qx,\ Q\succ 0\)

With \(f(x)=\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Qx\) and \(Q\succ 0\) we computed \(f^{*}(y)=\tfrac12 y^{\mathsf T}Q^{-1}y\) in §3.3.1. Substituting into Fenchel's inequality gives, for all \(x,y\),

\[ x^{\mathsf T}y\ \le\ \tfrac12\,x^{\mathsf T}Qx+\tfrac12\,y^{\mathsf T}Q^{-1}y . \]

Every conjugate pair you know yields a free inequality this way; this one is the generalized arithmetic–geometric mean bound of linear algebra.

!

The direction of the inequality is the trap. It is \(f(x)+f^{*}(y)\ge x^{\mathsf T}y\) — the two functions sum to at least the inner product. Equality holds exactly when this particular \(x\) attains the supremum defining \(f^{*}(y)\); in the smooth case, exactly when \(y=\nabla f(x)\) (§3).

§2the biconjugate

\(f^{**}=f\) — with a technical condition attached

The conjugate of the conjugate, \(f^{**}\), is again a function of \(x\). One might hope it always returns \(f\). It does — provided a technical condition holds.

If \(f\) is convex and closed (i.e. \(\operatorname{epi}f\) is a closed set; see §A.3.3), then \(f^{**}=f\). B&V §3.3.2 · L4861–4866

In particular, for a convex \(f\), \(\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbf{R}^{n}\) implies \(f\) is closed, and hence \(f^{**}=f\) (exercise 3.39). That is the case you meet most often, which is why the closedness hypothesis is easy to forget.

  • why closed
    Closedness of \(\operatorname{epi}f\) rules out functions that dip at a boundary point of their domain in a way no affine minorant can see. The conjugate only ever "sees" \(f\) through affine functions lying below it, so a non-closed dip is invisible and cannot be recovered.
  • why convex
    \(f^{**}\) is itself a conjugate — of \(f^{*}\) — and every conjugate is a pointwise supremum of affine functions of its argument. So \(f^{**}\) is always closed and convex, no matter what \(f\) is. A nonconvex \(f\) therefore cannot equal its own biconjugate.
  • what you get instead
    When \(f\) fails either hypothesis, \(f^{**}\) is not garbage: it is \(f\)'s closed convex hull — the largest closed convex function that lies below \(f\), equivalently the pointwise sup of all affine functions minorizing \(f\).
!

Both hypotheses matter, and they fail in different ways. \(f^{**}\) is always closed-convex, so a nonconvex \(f\) — even one defined on all of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) — does not satisfy \(f^{**}=f\); the biconjugate flattens it into its convex hull. And a convex \(f\) with a non-closed epigraph loses only the boundary value, which \(f^{**}\) fills in with the lower limit.

§3differentiable functions

The Legendre transform

The conjugate of a differentiable function \(f\) is also called the Legendre transform of \(f\). (To distinguish it, the general definition \(f^{*}(y)=\sup_x(y^{\mathsf T}x-f(x))\) is sometimes called the Fenchel conjugate.) B&V §3.3.2 · L4870–4884

Suppose \(f\) is convex and differentiable, with \(\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbf{R}^{n}\). Then the maximizer of \(y^{\mathsf T}x-f(x)\) is characterized by a gradient condition, and the value of the conjugate can be read off from it.

Claim — \(x^{*}\) maximizes \(y^{\mathsf T}x-f(x)\) iff \(y=\nabla f(x^{*})\)stationarity, both directions
\[ \nabla_x\bigl(y^{\mathsf T}x-f(x)\bigr)\Big|_{x=x^{*}}=0 \iff y=\nabla f(x^{*}) \]
why the objective is concave and differentiable on all of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\), so a stationary point is a global maximizer — and conversely a maximizer is stationary.
\[ f^{*}(y)=y^{\mathsf T}x^{*}-f(x^{*})=x^{*\mathsf T}\nabla f(x^{*})-f(x^{*}) \]
why substitute \(y=\nabla f(x^{*})\) into the definition. The sup is attained, so the value is exact.
→

The practical form. Do not try to invert \(\nabla f\) globally. Instead: pick any \(z\), set \(y=\nabla f(z)\), and read off \[ f^{*}(y)=z^{\mathsf T}\nabla f(z)-f(z). \] This parametrizes \(f^{*}\) by \(z\) rather than \(y\); solve the gradient equation \(y=\nabla f(z)\) for \(z\) wherever you can, and where you cannot, the parametric form still gives you the conjugate.

Recovers Example 3.22\(f(x)=\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Qx,\ Q\succ 0\)

Here the gradient equation is solvable in closed form: \(\nabla f(z)=Qz=y\Rightarrow z=Q^{-1}y\). Then

\[ f^{*}(y)=z^{\mathsf T}\nabla f(z)-f(z)=y^{\mathsf T}Q^{-1}y-\tfrac12 y^{\mathsf T}Q^{-1}y=\tfrac12\,y^{\mathsf T}Q^{-1}y, \]

which is exactly the conjugate computed in §3.3.1 — now obtained by one differentiation instead of a supremum argument.

!

The hypotheses are doing real work: convexity makes stationarity sufficient, differentiability makes \(\nabla f\) exist, and \(\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbf{R}^{n}\) means there is no boundary at which the sup could be attained without stationarity. Drop \(\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbf{R}^{n}\) and you must check the boundary; drop differentiability and \(\nabla f(x)\) becomes a set of slopes (visible as the staircase in Lab 1).

§4calculus rules I

Scaling and composition with an affine map

Scaling and offset. For \(a>0\) and \(b\in\mathbf{R}\), if \(g(x)=a f(x)+b\), then \(g^{*}(y)=a f^{*}(y/a)-b\). B&V §3.3.2 · L4886–4888
Composition with an affine map. Suppose \(A\in\mathbf{R}^{n\times n}\) is nonsingular and \(b\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\). If \(g(x)=f(Ax+b)\), then \[ g^{*}(y)=f^{*}(A^{-\mathsf T}y)-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y, \] with \(\operatorname{dom}g^{*}=A^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{dom}f^{*}\). B&V §3.3.2 · L4889–4891
Where the three pieces of the affine rule come fromsubstitute \(u=Ax+b\)
\[ g^{*}(y)=\sup_x\bigl(y^{\mathsf T}x-f(Ax+b)\bigr),\qquad u=Ax+b,\ \ x=A^{-1}(u-b) \]
why \(A\) nonsingular, so \(x\mapsto u\) is a bijection and the sup over \(x\) becomes a sup over \(u\).
\[ y^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}(u-b)=(A^{-\mathsf T}y)^{\mathsf T}u-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y \]
why moving \(A^{-1}\) across the inner product transposes it — this is where \(A^{-\mathsf T}\) is born, and where the constant \(-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y\) splits off.
\[ g^{*}(y)=\sup_u\bigl((A^{-\mathsf T}y)^{\mathsf T}u-f(u)\bigr)-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y=f^{*}(A^{-\mathsf T}y)-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y \]
why the constant leaves the sup. Finiteness now requires \(A^{-\mathsf T}y\in\operatorname{dom}f^{*}\), i.e. \(y\in A^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{dom}f^{*}\).
!

Watch the details. Three things are routinely mis-stated: it is the inverse transpose \(A^{-\mathsf T}\) on the argument — not \(A^{-1}\), not \(A^{\mathsf T}\); the correction term \(-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y\) is subtracted and is linear in \(y\); and the domain transforms by \(A^{\mathsf T}\) (not by \(A^{-\mathsf T}\)), \(\operatorname{dom}g^{*}=A^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{dom}f^{*}\). In the scaling rule the analogous traps are the \(y/a\) inside \(f^{*}\) (not \(ay\)) and the \(-b\) (the offset flips sign).

§5calculus rules II

The conjugate of a sum of independent functions

If \(f(u,v)=f_{1}(u)+f_{2}(v)\), where \(f_{1}\) and \(f_{2}\) are convex functions with conjugates \(f_{1}^{*}\) and \(f_{2}^{*}\), respectively, then \[ f^{*}(w,z)=f_{1}^{*}(w)+f_{2}^{*}(z). \] B&V §3.3.2 · L4893–4900

Here \(f_{1}\) and \(f_{2}\) are functions of different variables — the variable of \(f\) is the stacked pair \((u,v)\), and the dual variable is the stacked pair \((w,z)\).

Claim — the joint sup separatesindependence is the hypothesis
\[ f^{*}(w,z)=\sup_{u,v}\Bigl(w^{\mathsf T}u+z^{\mathsf T}v-f_{1}(u)-f_{2}(v)\Bigr) \]
why the definition, with the inner product on the product space splitting as \(w^{\mathsf T}u+z^{\mathsf T}v\).
\[ =\sup_{u}\bigl(w^{\mathsf T}u-f_{1}(u)\bigr)+\sup_{v}\bigl(z^{\mathsf T}v-f_{2}(v)\bigr)=f_{1}^{*}(w)+f_{2}^{*}(z) \]
why the objective splits into a term in \(u\) alone plus a term in \(v\) alone, and \(u,v\) range independently — so the joint sup is the sum of two separate sups.
!

The word "independent" is essential. The separation in the crux step is legitimate only because \(u\) and \(v\) are distinct variables free to be optimized apart from one another. For two convex functions of the same variable, \(f=f_{1}+f_{2}\), the term-by-term rule fails — the conjugate of a sum is not the sum of the conjugates, and the correct statement requires a different, more delicate construction.

§6expert lens · diagnose

What mastery looks like here

01Fenchel

\(f(x)+f^{*}(y)\ge x^{\mathsf T}y\) for all \(x,y\) — one line from the definition. Equality iff this \(x\) attains the sup defining \(f^{*}(y)\); in the smooth case, iff \(y=\nabla f(x)\). Instance: \(x^{\mathsf T}y\le\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Qx+\tfrac12 y^{\mathsf T}Q^{-1}y\).

02Biconjugate

\(f^{**}=f\) iff \(f\) is convex and closed (in particular, convex with \(\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbf{R}^{n}\)). \(f^{**}\) is always closed-convex — so in general it is \(f\)'s closed convex hull, not \(f\).

03Legendre

For convex differentiable \(f\) on \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\): with \(y=\nabla f(z)\), \(f^{*}(y)=z^{\mathsf T}\nabla f(z)-f(z)\). Solve the gradient equation where possible; no global inverse of \(\nabla f\) is assumed.

04Calculus rules (exact)

\(af+b\mapsto a f^{*}(y/a)-b\); \(f(Ax+b)\mapsto f^{*}(A^{-\mathsf T}y)-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y\) with \(\operatorname{dom}=A^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{dom}f^{*}\); \(f_{1}(u)+f_{2}(v)\mapsto f_{1}^{*}(w)+f_{2}^{*}(z)\) for independent variables.

05Traps

The direction of Fenchel's \(\ge\); dropping "closed" or "convex" from \(f^{**}=f\); writing \(A^{-1}\) or \(A^{\mathsf T}\) instead of \(A^{-\mathsf T}\); forgetting \(-b^{\mathsf T}A^{-\mathsf T}y\) or the \(A^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{dom}f^{*}\) domain; applying the sum rule to functions of the same variable.
