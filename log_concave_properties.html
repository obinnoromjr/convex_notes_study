Log-concavity: what survives

The calculus of the class. A second-order test, then the one fact worth memorising: log-concavity is not closed under addition, but it is closed under integration — and that single closure rule delivers marginals, convolutions, CDFs, yields and volumes.

log-concave \(\iff f\nabla^{2}f \preceq \nabla f\nabla f^{T}\) sums: log-concavity fails integrals: log-concavity holds p. 105–108 · mastery: diagnose
§0where we are

From the definition to the calculus

§3.5.1 defined the two classes: \(f\) is log-concave when \(f>0\) and \(\log f\) is concave, log-convex when \(\log f\) is convex. That subsection told us what the classes are. This one tells us how to work with them — which is what turns a definition into a tool.

Three things, in order. A second-order test, so that log-concavity can be checked by differentiating twice instead of verifying a chord inequality. A closure table — which operations keep you inside each class — whose defining feature is an asymmetry: products preserve both, sums preserve only log-convexity, and integration preserves only log-concavity. And then the payoffs, all of which are consequences of that last, deepest closure rule: marginals, convolutions, the Laplace transform, cumulative distribution functions, manufacturing yields, and the volume of a polyhedron as a function of its right-hand side.

§1twice-differentiable characterization

Differentiate the logarithm twice

A twice differentiable function \(f\) with convex domain is log-concave if and only if \[ f(x)\,\nabla^{2}f(x) \;\preceq\; \nabla f(x)\,\nabla f(x)^{T} \] for all \(x\in\mathop{\bf dom} f\), and log-convex if and only if the inequality holds with \(\succeq\). B&V §3.5.2 · L5426–5434

The test comes from one differentiation. Since \(f>0\) on its domain, \(\log f\) is twice differentiable wherever \(f\) is, and the chain rule gives

\[ \nabla^{2}\log f(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{f(x)}\nabla^{2}f(x)\;-\;\frac{1}{f(x)^{2}}\nabla f(x)\nabla f(x)^{T}. \]
Claim  the matrix inequality is exactly \(\nabla^{2}\log f\preceq 0\)clear the positive factor
\[ \nabla \log f = \frac{\nabla f}{f} \]
why chain rule on \(\log\). Note the gradient of \(\log f\) is the gradient of \(f\) rescaled — the relative gradient.
\[ \nabla^{2}\log f = \frac{\nabla^{2} f}{f} - \frac{\nabla f\,\nabla f^{T}}{f^{2}} \]
why differentiate the quotient \(\nabla f/f\) once more; the second term is the derivative of the \(1/f\) factor and is a rank-one matrix.
\[ \nabla^{2}\log f \preceq 0 \;\iff\; f\,\nabla^{2}f \preceq \nabla f\,\nabla f^{T} \]
why multiply through by the scalar \(f(x)^{2}>0\). Multiplying a matrix inequality by a positive scalar preserves it — this is the only place positivity of \(f\) is used, and it is what makes the test clean.
i

Why state it as \(f\nabla^{2}f\preceq\nabla f\nabla f^{T}\) rather than \(\nabla^{2}\log f\preceq0\). The cleared form involves only derivatives of \(f\) itself, so you never have to form \(\log f\) symbolically; and it stays meaningful at points where \(f\) is smooth but \(\log f\) is awkward to write down. Note also that the right-hand side \(\nabla f\nabla f^{T}\) is always positive semidefinite and of rank at most one — so log-concavity permits \(\nabla^{2}f\) to have one direction of positive curvature, funded by the gradient.

In one variable the matrices are numbers and the test collapses to a memorable inequality:

  • scalar
    \(f\) is log-concave \(\iff f(x)f''(x)\le f'(x)^{2}\), and log-convex \(\iff f(x)f''(x)\ge f'(x)^{2}\), for all \(x\in\mathop{\bf dom} f\).
  • reading it
    Curvature is allowed, but only in proportion to the square of the slope. Where \(f'=0\) (a critical point) log-concavity forces \(f''\le0\): a log-concave function can have no interior local minimum unless it is flat there.
  • both
    Equality \(ff''=(f')^{2}\) everywhere means \(\nabla^{2}\log f=0\), i.e. \(\log f\) affine, i.e. \(f(x)=e^{a^{T}x+b}\) — the functions that are simultaneously log-concave and log-convex.
!

Direction check. The \(\preceq\) sign goes with log-concave — which is the reverse of the naive reflex "concave ↔ small". Anchor it on the Gaussian: \(f=e^{-x^{2}/2}\) has \(ff''=(x^{2}-1)e^{-x^{2}}\) and \((f')^{2}=x^{2}e^{-x^{2}}\), so \(ff''-(f')^{2}=-e^{-x^{2}}<0\). Log-concave, and the scalar test reads \(\le\).

§2multiplication and addition

The closure asymmetry

Log-convexity and log-concavity are closed under multiplication and positive scaling. If \(f\) and \(g\) are log-concave, then so is the pointwise product \(h(x)=f(x)g(x)\); if \(f\) and \(g\) are log-convex, so is \(fg\). B&V §3.5.2 · L5436–5449

This one is immediate and worth seeing as a one-liner: \(\log(fg)=\log f+\log g\), and a sum of two concave functions is concave (a sum of two convex functions is convex). Positive scaling is the special case \(g\equiv c>0\), whose logarithm is a constant. Products are where log-concavity is at its most comfortable — which is precisely why the class is the right one for probability densities, which multiply under independence.

Simple examples show that the sum of log-concave functions is not, in general, log-concave. Log-convexity, however, is preserved under sums: let \(f\) and \(g\) be log-convex functions, i.e. \(F=\log f\) and \(G=\log g\) are convex. Then \(\log\bigl(f+g\bigr)=\log\bigl(e^{F}+e^{G}\bigr)\) is convex, since the log-sum-exp function is convex and nondecreasing in each argument, and \(F,G\) are convex. B&V §3.5.2 · L5436–5449
Claim  \(f,g\) log-convex \(\Rightarrow f+g\) log-convexcomposition rule, §3.2.4
\[ \log(f+g) = \log\bigl(e^{F}+e^{G}\bigr),\qquad F=\log f,\ G=\log g \]
why rewrite each summand as \(e^{\text{something convex}}\). This is the whole idea: the sum lives inside a log-sum-exp.
\[ h(u,v)=\log(e^{u}+e^{v})\ \text{is convex and nondecreasing in each argument} \]
why log-sum-exp is convex (§3.1.5) and \(\partial h/\partial u=e^{u}/(e^{u}+e^{v})>0\). Both properties are needed for the next line.
\[ h\bigl(F(x),G(x)\bigr)\ \text{convex} \]
why the vector composition rule: \(h\) convex, nondecreasing in each argument, composed with convex \(F,G\), is convex. Monotonicity is where log-convexity of the pieces gets used.
!

The asymmetry, stated once. Products preserve both classes. Sums preserve only log-convexity. This is the single most examinable fact in the subsection, and the error to avoid is stating it backwards — "log-concavity is closed under sums" is false, and the counterexample is one line away.

The counterexample: two separated bumps

Take two Gaussian bumps with means \(\pm a\),

\[ f(x)=e^{-(x-a)^{2}/2}+e^{-(x+a)^{2}/2}. \]

Each summand is log-concave — the logarithm of each is a downward parabola. Their sum need not be. Differentiating \(\log f\) twice at the symmetric point \(x=0\) gives

\[ (\log f)''(0)\;=\;a^{2}-1, \]

so as soon as the bumps are separated by more than one standard deviation, \(a>1\), the logarithm is strictly convex at the midpoint and \(f\) is not log-concave. At \(a=3\) the number is \(a^{2}-1=8\): the valley between the two peaks is deep, and the chord joining the peaks sits far above the graph — exactly the picture in the header.

Counterexample · verified\((\log f)''(0)=a^{2}-1\)

Symmetry does the work. At \(x=0\) write \(u=e^{-(a^{2})/2}\) for the common value of each summand. Then \(f(0)=2u\), \(f'(0)=0\) by symmetry, and \(f''(0)=2u(a^{2}-1)\). The scalar test compares \(f f''=4u^{2}(a^{2}-1)\) with \((f')^{2}=0\), so

\[ (\log f)''(0)=\frac{f f''-(f')^{2}}{f^{2}}\bigg|_{0}=\frac{4u^{2}(a^{2}-1)}{4u^{2}}=a^{2}-1. \]

Separation matters, and the threshold is sharp. For \(a\le1\) the bumps overlap enough that \(a^{2}-1\le0\) and this test is passed at the midpoint; for \(a>1\) log-concavity fails. The counterexample is not "sums are bad" — it is "sums are bad once the pieces are far apart".

Do not over-read the failure. Sums of log-concave functions are not always non-log-concave — the lab's \(a\le1\) regime is log-concave, and a sum of two identical log-concave functions is just a positive multiple of one of them, hence log-concave. The claim is only that log-concavity is not preserved: closure means "for all", so one counterexample settles it.

§3integration

Integration — the deep closure rule

Addition of two functions destroys log-concavity. Integration — which is, after all, a limit of sums — preserves it. That is not a contradiction but a genuinely surprising theorem, and the difference is the hypothesis: the integrand must be log-concave jointly in the variable being kept and the variable being integrated out.

The easy half: log-convexity

If \(f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{m}\to\mathbf{R}\) is log-convex in \(x\) for each \(y\), then \[ g(x)=\int_{C} f(x,y)\,dy \] is log-convex. B&V §3.5.2 · L5450–5514

This is the sum result taken to the limit. A finite sum \(\sum_{i} f(x,y_{i})\) of functions log-convex in \(x\) is log-convex by §2; the integral is a limit of such sums, and log-convexity is preserved under pointwise limits. Notice the weak hypothesis: log-convexity in \(x\) separately, for each fixed \(y\), is enough. Nothing joint is required.

The deep half: log-concavity

If \(f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{m}\to\mathbf{R}\) is log-concave, then \[ g(x)=\int f(x,y)\,dy \] is a log-concave function of \(x\) (on \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\)). B&V §3.5.2 · L5450–5514
!

Read the hypothesis carefully. Here \(f\) must be log-concave as a function of the pair \((x,y)\) — jointly, on \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{m}\) — not merely log-concave in \(x\) for each fixed \(y\). The separated-bumps counterexample of §2 is exactly a case where the summands are log-concave in \(x\) for each index but not jointly, and the conclusion fails. Joint log-concavity is what buys the theorem.

i

On the proof. B&V do not prove this result; it is a nontrivial theorem and they refer the reader to the references (it is due to Prékopa; the associated inequality is the Prékopa–Leindler inequality, which is the functional form of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality). What is expected of us here is fluent use of the statement, not a reproduction of its proof.

What the theorem immediately gives

  • marginals
    If \(p(x,y)\) is a log-concave joint density, then the marginal \(p(x)=\int p(x,y)\,dy\) is log-concave. Log-concavity is inherited by marginal distributions.
  • convolution
    The convolution of two log-concave functions is log-concave: \((f*g)(x)=\int f(x-y)g(y)\,dy\), and the integrand \(f(x-y)g(y)\) is log-concave jointly in \((x,y)\) — the map \((x,y)\mapsto x-y\) is affine, and log-concavity survives composition with an affine map (§3.5.1) and multiplication (§2).
  • probability
    If \(C\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{n}\) is convex and \(w\) is a random vector with log-concave density \(p\), then \(\;\mathop{\bf prob}(x+w\in C)\;\) is a log-concave function of \(x\).
Claim  \(f(x)=\mathop{\bf prob}(x+w\in C)\) is log-concavethe standard packaging move
\[ f(x)=\int g(x+w)\,p(w)\,dw,\qquad g(u)=\begin{cases}1 & u\in C\\ 0 & u\notin C\end{cases} \]
why write the probability as an integral of an indicator. This is the move that turns every "probability of landing in a set" question into the integration theorem.
\[ g\ \text{log-concave} \iff C\ \text{convex} \]
why the indicator of \(C\), with the convention \(\log 0=-\infty\), has \(\log g\) equal to \(0\) on \(C\) and \(-\infty\) off it — a concave extended-value function exactly when \(C\) is convex. This is where convexity of \(C\) is used, and it is the only place.
\[ (x,w)\mapsto g(x+w)p(w)\ \text{is log-concave jointly} \]
why \(g(x+w)\) is log-concave in \((x,w)\) because \((x,w)\mapsto x+w\) is affine; \(p(w)\) is log-concave in \((x,w)\) by hypothesis; and the product of log-concave functions is log-concave (§2).
\[ \int g(x+w)p(w)\,dw\ \text{is log-concave in }x \]
why integrate out \(w\) and apply the theorem. All three preservation rules — affine composition, product, integration — are used in sequence.

That four-line argument is a template, not a one-off. Sections 5, 6 and 7 below are the same four lines with a different set \(C\) and a different density.

§4example 3.41

Laplace transform, moment generating function, cumulant generating function

Example 3.41  Suppose \(p:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}\) satisfies \(p(x)\ge0\) for all \(x\). The Laplace transform \[ P(z)=\int p(x)\,e^{-z^{T}x}\,dx \] is log-convex on \(\mathop{\bf dom} P=\{z\mid P(z)<\infty\}\) (which is a convex set). B&V §3.5.2 · L5457–5478

This is the log-convex integration rule applied with the parameter \(z\) as the kept variable and \(x\) integrated out. For each fixed \(x\), the integrand as a function of \(z\) is

\[ \log\bigl(p(x)e^{-z^{T}x}\bigr) = \log p(x) - z^{T}x, \]

which is affine in \(z\), hence convex — so each integrand is log-convex in \(z\), and the integral inherits log-convexity. Note how little is asked of \(p\): only nonnegativity. \(p\) need not be log-concave, log-convex, or anything else.

i

The domain is convex for free. \(\log P\) is a convex function; the set where a convex function is finite is its domain, and domains of convex functions are convex. So \(\{z\mid P(z)<\infty\}\) is convex without any separate argument.

Specialising to a probability density

Now suppose \(p\) is the density of a random variable \(v\). Then \(P(-z)=\mathop{\bf E}e^{z^{T}v}\) is the moment generating function \(M(z)\), and \(\log M\) is the cumulant generating function. Since \(M(z)=P(-z)\) is \(P\) composed with an affine map, \(M\) is log-convex too — so \(\log M\) is convex.

Derivatives at the originmoments and cumulants

Differentiating under the integral, at \(z=0\) (where \(M(0)=1\)):

\[ \nabla M(0)=\mathop{\bf E}v,\qquad \nabla^{2}M(0)=\mathop{\bf E}vv^{T}, \]

and for the cumulant generating function,

\[ \nabla \log M(0)=\mathop{\bf E}v,\qquad \nabla^{2}\log M(0)=\mathop{\bf E}vv^{T}-\bigl(\mathop{\bf E}v\bigr)\bigl(\mathop{\bf E}v\bigr)^{T}=\mathop{\bf cov}(v). \]

The second identity is the second-order log-convexity formula of §1 evaluated at \(0\): \(\nabla^{2}\log M = \nabla^{2}M/M - \nabla M\nabla M^{T}/M^{2}\), and \(M(0)=1\). Convexity of \(\log M\) therefore says, at the origin, that \(\mathop{\bf cov}(v)\succeq0\) — the covariance matrix is positive semidefinite. A familiar fact, recovered as a special case of a curvature statement.

!

Worth keeping. The CGF is convex, its gradient at \(0\) is the mean, its Hessian at \(0\) is the covariance. This is the cleanest example in the section of log-convexity carrying real statistical content rather than being a formal property.

§5example 3.42

The cumulative distribution function of a log-concave density

Example 3.42  Let \(f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}\) be a log-concave probability density, and let \(C\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{n}\) be a convex set. Then the function \[ F(x)=\mathop{\bf prob}(w\preceq x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}\!\!\cdots\!\int_{-\infty}^{x_{n}} f(z)\,dz \] is log-concave. In particular, the cumulative distribution function of a log-concave density is log-concave. B&V §3.5.2 · L5516–5530

The proof is the packaging template of §3 with \(C\) taken to be the (convex) set \(\{z\mid z\preceq x\}\). Write

\[ F(x)=\int \Psi(x,z)\,f(z)\,dz,\qquad \Psi(x,z)=\begin{cases}1 & z\preceq x\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \]

and check the two ingredients. \(\Psi\) is the indicator of the set \(\{(x,z)\mid z\preceq x\}\), which is convex — indeed it is a cone defined by the linear inequalities \(z_{i}-x_{i}\le0\) — so \(\Psi\) is log-concave jointly in \((x,z)\). The density \(f(z)\) is log-concave in \((x,z)\) by hypothesis. Their product is log-concave, and integrating out \(z\) leaves a log-concave function of \(x\) .

i

Where the joint hypothesis bites. \(\Psi\) is the reason the theorem's joint log-concavity requirement is not a technicality to be waved through: \(\Psi(x,z)\) genuinely couples the two variables, and it is log-concave only because \(\{(x,z)\mid z\preceq x\}\) is a convex set. Change \(\preceq\) to something non-convex and the argument stops.

The special case everyone knows\(\Phi\) is log-concave

Take \(n=1\) and \(f\) the standard normal density \(e^{-z^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\), which is log-concave (its logarithm is \(-z^{2}/2-\tfrac12\log 2\pi\), a downward parabola). The theorem says the Gaussian CDF

\[ \Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz \]

is log-concave — the fact quoted without proof in §3.5.1's list of examples. Here it is, as a one-line consequence of the integration theorem. The same argument covers the CDF of any log-concave density: exponential, uniform, Laplace, logistic, and so on.

§6example 3.43

Yield function

Example 3.43  Let \(x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\) be a vector of nominal parameter values for a product, \(w\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\) a random vector of manufacturing variations, and \(S\) the set of acceptable parameter values. The yield of the product, as a function of the nominal parameter values, is \[ Y(x)=\mathop{\bf prob}(x+w\in S). \] If \(S\) is convex and \(w\) has a log-concave probability density \(p\), then \(Y\) is a log-concave function of \(x\). B&V §3.5.2 · L5533–5551

Same template again: write \(Y(x)=\int g(x+w)p(w)\,dw\) with \(g\) the indicator of \(S\), note that \(g\) is log-concave because \(S\) is convex, that \(g(x+w)\) is log-concave in \((x,w)\) because \(x+w\) is affine in \((x,w)\), that the product with \(p(w)\) is log-concave, and integrate.

The payoff: yield regions are convex

It follows that the \(\alpha\)-yield region, defined as the set of nominal parameters for which the yield exceeds \(\alpha\), \[ \{x \mid Y(x)\ge\alpha\}=\{x\mid \log Y(x)\ge\log\alpha\}, \] is convex for \(\alpha>0\), since it is a superlevel set of the concave function \(\log Y\). B&V §3.5.2 · L5533–5551
!

Why this matters for optimization. "Design so that at least \(\alpha\) of the units come out acceptable" is a convex constraint — it can be written \(\log\alpha-\log Y(x)\le0\) with the left side convex. Without log-concavity, a yield requirement would be an arbitrary nonconvex constraint. The restriction \(\alpha>0\) is needed only so that \(\log\alpha\) is finite; at \(\alpha=0\) the set is all of \(\mathbf{R}^{n}\) anyway.

Both hypotheses are load-bearing. Switch the lab's acceptable set to two disjoint intervals — a non-convex \(S\) — and \(\log Y\) develops a convex valley between the two acceptable windows, exactly like the two-bump counterexample of §2. The \(\alpha\)-yield region then splits into two pieces and is no longer convex. Log-concavity of the density is equally necessary: a bimodal \(w\) would do the same damage from the other side.

§7example 3.44

Volume of a polyhedron

Example 3.44  Let \(A\in\mathbf{R}^{m\times n}\). Then the function \[ \mathop{\bf vol}P_{u},\qquad P_{u}=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\mid Ax\preceq u\}, \] is a log-concave function of \(u\). B&V §3.5.2 · L5556–5571

The polyhedron's shape is fixed by \(A\); only the right-hand side \(u\) moves, sliding each face in or out along its own normal. The claim is that the volume swept out is log-concave in that right-hand side.

Claim  \(u\mapsto\mathop{\bf vol}P_{u}\) is log-concaveindicator + integrate out \(x\)
\[ \Psi(x,u)=\begin{cases}1 & Ax\preceq u\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]
why the indicator of \(\{(x,u)\mid Ax\preceq u\}\) — a set cut out by the linear inequalities \(a_{i}^{T}x-u_{i}\le0\) in the pair \((x,u)\), hence convex, hence \(\Psi\) log-concave jointly.
\[ \int \Psi(x,u)\,dx = \mathop{\bf vol}P_{u} \]
why integrating an indicator over \(x\) measures the slice — here the volume of \(P_{u}\). Note which variable is integrated out: \(x\), the one we do not keep.
\[ \Rightarrow\ \mathop{\bf vol}P_{u}\ \text{log-concave in }u \]
why the integration theorem, with the roles of the two variables as above. No density is involved at all — the log-concave integrand is a bare indicator.
i

What this is really saying. It is a Brunn–Minkowski statement in disguise: \(P_{\theta u+(1-\theta)v}\supseteq\theta P_{u}+(1-\theta)P_{v}\), so the volume of the polyhedron at an averaged right-hand side is at least the volume of the Minkowski average of the two polyhedra, and Brunn–Minkowski makes that at least \((\mathop{\bf vol}P_{u})^{\theta}(\mathop{\bf vol}P_{v})^{1-\theta}\) — the geometric-mean form of log-concavity from §3.5.1.

§8expert lens

Diagnose: what to have loaded

01 The second-order test, both directions

Log-concave \(\iff f\nabla^{2}f\preceq\nabla f\nabla f^{T}\); log-convex \(\iff\succeq\). Scalar form: \(ff''\le(f')^{2}\) for log-concave, \(\ge\) for log-convex. Derive it, if in doubt, from \(\nabla^{2}\log f=\nabla^{2}f/f-\nabla f\nabla f^{T}/f^{2}\) and multiply by \(f^{2}>0\).

02 The closure table is the crux

Product and positive scaling: both classes closed. Sum: log-convex only — via log-sum-exp of convex functions. Integration and marginals: log-concave (the deep theorem, joint hypothesis). Log-convexity also survives integration under the weaker, separate hypothesis.

03 Carry one counterexample

\(f(x)=e^{-(x-3)^{2}/2}+e^{-(x+3)^{2}/2}\), with \((\log f)''(0)=a^{2}-1=8>0\) at \(a=3\). Separation is the mechanism: means \(\pm a\) with \(a\le1\) give \(a^{2}-1\le0\) and no failure at the midpoint.

04 The integration payoffs, as a list

Marginals of log-concave densities; convolution; \(\mathop{\bf prob}(x+w\in C)\) for convex \(C\); CDFs (Ex 3.42, of which \(\Phi\) is the special case); yield \(Y(x)=\mathop{\bf prob}(x+w\in S)\) (Ex 3.43); \(\mathop{\bf vol}P_{u}\) (Ex 3.44). All log-concave — and all \(\alpha\)-superlevel regions therefore convex.

05 Laplace, MGF, CGF

\(P(z)=\int p(x)e^{-z^{T}x}dx\) is log-convex on \(\{z\mid P(z)<\infty\}\) for any \(p\ge0\), because \(\log p(x)-z^{T}x\) is affine in \(z\). Hence the CGF \(\log M\) is convex, with \(\nabla\log M(0)=\mathop{\bf E}v\) and \(\nabla^{2}\log M(0)=\mathop{\bf cov}(v)\).

06 Traps to say out loud

Don't claim log-concavity is closed under sums — log-convexity is. Don't weaken the integration hypothesis to "log-concave in \(x\) for each \(y\)" — it must be joint. Don't flip the twice-differentiable direction: \(\preceq\) is log-concave.

Summary — main points for quick review

Log-concave and log-convex functions: properties

twice differentiable

Log-concave \(\iff f\nabla^{2}f\preceq\nabla f\nabla f^{T}\) (scalar: \(ff''\le(f')^{2}\)); log-convex \(\iff\succeq\). From \(\nabla^{2}\log f=\nabla^{2}f/f-\nabla f\nabla f^{T}/f^{2}\), cleared by \(f^{2}>0\).

closure asymmetry

Product and positive scaling: both. Sum: log-convex only — \(e^{-(x-3)^{2}/2}+e^{-(x+3)^{2}/2}\) has \((\log f)''(0)=8>0\). Integration and marginals: log-concave (deep theorem, joint hypothesis).

integration payoffs

Marginals, convolution, \(\mathop{\bf prob}(x+w\in C)\), CDF, yield \(Y(x)=\mathop{\bf prob}(x+w\in S)\), \(\mathop{\bf vol}P_{u}\) — all log-concave; \(\alpha\)-superlevel regions convex for \(\alpha>0\).

Laplace / MGF / CGF (Ex 3.41)

\(P(z)=\int p\,e^{-z^{T}x}dx\) log-convex on \(\{P<\infty\}\) since \(\log p(x)-z^{T}x\) is affine in \(z\). CGF \(\log M\) convex, \(\nabla\log M(0)=\mathop{\bf E}v\), \(\nabla^{2}\log M(0)=\mathop{\bf cov}(v)\).
