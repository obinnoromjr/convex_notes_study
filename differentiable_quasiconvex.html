Differentiable quasiconvex functions

Calculus characterizations, and the two places quasiconvexity parts company with convexity: the gradient only fixes a direction, and a zero gradient certifies nothing.

§0where we are

From the modified Jensen inequality to calculus

§3.4.2 characterized quasiconvexity by the modified Jensen inequality (3.19): on any segment the value never exceeds the larger of the two endpoint values. That is a statement about pairs of points. This subsection gives the calculus characterizations for differentiable \(f\) — the analogs of the first- and second-order conditions for convexity — together with the important ways quasiconvexity differs from convexity. Two of those differences matter more than anything else here: the first-order condition constrains only a direction, not a value; and a vanishing gradient is not a certificate of optimality.

§1first-order condition

(3.20) — a sign condition on the directional derivative

Suppose \(f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\) is differentiable. Then \(f\) is quasiconvex if and only if \(\mathrm{dom}\,f\) is convex and for all \(x,y\in\mathrm{dom}\,f\), \[ f(y)\le f(x)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)\le 0. \] B&V §3.4.3 · L5130–5134 · (3.20)

Read it as an implication with a premise you must check first. Only pairs with \(f(y)\le f(x)\) say anything; for pairs with \(f(y)>f(x)\) the condition is silent, and the inner product may have either sign.

The contrast with the convexity condition (3.2) is the point of the whole subsection:

  • convex
    \(f(y)\ \ge\ f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)\) for all \(x,y\). The gradient produces a global affine underestimator — a numerical lower bound on \(f(y)\), valid everywhere.
  • quasiconvex
    \(f(y)\le f(x)\ \Rightarrow\ \nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)\le 0\). The gradient produces no bound at all — only the statement that stepping from \(x\) toward \(y\) cannot have positive directional slope.

Equivalently: if \(y\) is no higher than \(x\), then moving from \(x\) in the direction \(y-x\) does not increase \(f\) to first order. Convexity bounds the value; quasiconvexity only tells you which direction does not go up. The proof of (3.20) is left as exercise 3.43.

!

The condition is not symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\), and the gradient is always taken at \(x\) — the point whose value is the larger of the two. Writing \(\nabla f(y)^{T}(y-x)\), or writing the convex bound \(f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T(y-x)\) and calling it quasiconvexity, are the two standard errors here.

the contrapositive, which is sometimes easier to usea strict ascent direction certifies a strictly higher value

Contrapositive of (3.20): \(\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x) > 0 \Rightarrow f(y) > f(x)\). If the direction \(y-x\) strictly ascends at \(x\), then \(y\) must sit strictly above \(x\). This is the form that shows up when you want to exclude points from a sublevel set.

§2geometric interpretation · figure 3.12

∇f(x) is the outward normal of a supporting hyperplane

The condition (3.20) has a simple geometric interpretation when \(\nabla f(x)\neq 0\). It states that \(\nabla f(x)\) defines a supporting hyperplane to the sublevel set \(\{y\mid f(y)\le f(x)\}\) at the point \(x\). B&V §3.4.3 · L5165–5167 · figure 3.12

Unpack it in two steps. Every \(y\) in the sublevel set \(\{y\mid f(y)\le f(x)\}\) satisfies the premise of (3.20), so it satisfies \(\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)\le 0\). That inequality describes exactly the halfspace

\[ H \;=\; \{\,y \mid \nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)\le 0\,\}, \]

whose bounding hyperplane \(\{y\mid \nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)=0\}\) passes through \(x\). So the entire sublevel set lies inside \(H\), and \(x\) itself lies on the boundary hyperplane. A hyperplane that contains a point of a set and has the whole set on one side of it is a supporting hyperplane — the §2.5.2 notion — and \(\nabla f(x)\) is its outward normal.

!

The gradient points out of the sublevel set, in the direction of increase, and the sublevel set sits in the halfspace opposite the gradient. Note also that \(x\) need not be a boundary point of the sublevel set for the statement to be interesting — but when \(\nabla f(x)\ne0\), it is: \(f\) increases along \(\nabla f(x)\), so points just past \(x\) in that direction have larger value and are excluded.

§3the key contrast

∇f(x) = 0 does not give a global minimizer

For a differentiable convex \(f\), condition (3.2) with \(\nabla f(x)=0\) collapses to \(f(y)\ge f(x)\) for all \(y\): the point \(x\) is a global minimizer. This single line is why unconstrained convex minimization is the search for a stationary point.

For quasiconvex functions this is not true: a point can satisfy \(\nabla f(x)=0\), yet not be a global (or even local) minimizer of \(f\). As an example consider the quasiconvex function \(f(x)=x^{3}\) on \(\mathbb{R}\), and the point \(x=0\): we have \(f'(0)=0\), but for all \(x<0\), \(f(x)<f(0)=0\). B&V §3.4.3 · L5169–5173

Why does the convex argument break? Because it used the value bound. For quasiconvex \(f\), setting \(\nabla f(x)=0\) in (3.20) yields \(0\le 0\) — the implication becomes vacuous and delivers no information about \(f(y)\) whatsoever. There is nothing left to conclude.

\(f(x)=x^{3}\) is quasiconvex because it is monotone (nondecreasing), and every monotone function on \(\mathbb{R}\) is quasiconvex: its sublevel sets \(\{x\mid x^{3}\le\alpha\}=(-\infty,\alpha^{1/3}]\) are intervals. Yet \(f'(0)=0\) while \(f\) decreases without bound to the left, \(f(x)\to-\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\). The stationary point at the origin is an inflection, not a minimum.

!

Moral. A zero gradient does not certify optimality for quasiconvex problems. Stationarity is not trustworthy — which is exactly why quasiconvex problems are solved by bisection on the sublevel sets (§4.2.5) rather than by hunting for \(\nabla f=0\).

a quasiconvex function with no critical points at allthe conditions can hold vacuously

Take \(f(t)=\arctan t\). Then \(f'(t)=1/(1+t^{2})>0\) everywhere, so there is no \(t\) with \(f'(t)=0\). The function is monotone, hence quasiconvex (indeed quasilinear), and it is neither convex nor concave. Both second-order conditions below quantify over directions \(y\) with \(y^{T}\nabla f(x)=0\); on \(\mathbb{R}\) that forces \(y=0\) whenever \(f'(x)\neq0\), so for \(\arctan\) the premise never fires and the conditions hold with nothing to check. It is a useful reminder that these are conditions at critical directions, not global curvature requirements.

§4second-order necessary condition

(3.21) — nonnegative curvature on the gradient's orthogonal complement

Now suppose \(f\) is twice differentiable. If \(f\) is quasiconvex, then for all \(x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f\) and all \(y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\), \[ y^{T}\nabla f(x)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x)\,y\;\ge\;0. \] B&V §3.4.3 · L5177–5188 · (3.21)

Three readings of the same statement, in increasing generality:

  • on ℝ
    The condition reduces to \(f'(x)=0\ \Rightarrow\ f''(x)\ge0\): wherever the slope vanishes, the curvature must be nonnegative. A quasiconvex function on \(\mathbb{R}\) may not have a strict local maximum in the interior of its domain.
  • at ∇f = 0
    If \(\nabla f(x)=0\) then every \(y\) satisfies the premise, so \(\nabla^{2}f(x)\succeq 0\). At a stationary point the Hessian must be positive semidefinite in full — the same requirement as for convexity, but only there.
  • at ∇f ≠ 0
    The premise picks out the hyperplane \(\nabla f(x)^{\perp}\), of dimension \(n-1\). The condition says \(\nabla^{2}f(x)\) is positive semidefinite on that subspace. A symmetric matrix that is PSD on an \((n-1)\)-dimensional subspace can have at most one negative eigenvalue.
!

"At most one negative eigenvalue" — not zero. This is precisely the slack that separates quasiconvexity from convexity in the second-order picture: a quasiconvex function is allowed one direction of negative curvature, provided that direction is not orthogonal to the gradient. Requiring zero negative eigenvalues would be requiring convexity.

the eigenvalue count in actionf(z) = −z₁z₂ on the positive orthant

Take \(f(z)=-z_{1}z_{2}\) on \(\{z\mid z_{1}>0,\ z_{2}>0\}\), the quasiconvex twin of the quasiconcave \(z_1z_2\). Then

\[ \nabla f(z)=\begin{bmatrix}-z_{2}\\-z_{1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \nabla^{2}f(z)=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lambda=\{+1,-1\}. \]

The Hessian has one negative eigenvalue, so \(f\) is certainly not convex. But the directions orthogonal to the gradient are the multiples of \(y=(z_{1},-z_{2})\), and

\[ y^{T}\nabla^{2}f(z)\,y \;=\; -2y_{1}y_{2} \;=\; 2z_{1}z_{2}\;>\;0 \quad\text{on the positive orthant.} \]

Strictly positive, so (3.22) below applies and \(f\) is quasiconvex there. The single negative eigenvalue is invisible to the test because its eigenvector \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) is never orthogonal to \(\nabla f(z)=-(z_{2},z_{1})\) when \(z\succ0\).

the disqualifierf(z) = z₁² − z₂², same eigenvalue signature, fails

The saddle \(f(z)=z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2}\) has \(\nabla f(z)=(2z_{1},-2z_{2})\) and \(\nabla^{2}f=\mathrm{diag}(2,-2)\), again exactly one negative eigenvalue. Yet the orthogonal direction is \(y=(z_{2},z_{1})\) and

\[ y^{T}\nabla^{2}f\,y \;=\; 2z_{2}^{2}-2z_{1}^{2}\;<\;0 \quad\text{whenever } |z_{1}|>|z_{2}|, \]

so (3.21) fails and \(f\) is not quasiconvex. Concretely, its sublevel set \(\{z\mid z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2}\le\alpha\}\) for \(\alpha>0\) is the nonconvex region between the two hyperbola branches. The eigenvalue count alone never settles the question — what matters is where the negative direction sits relative to \(\nabla f(x)^{\perp}\).

§5second-order sufficient condition

(3.22) — a partial converse, with strict inequality

A partial converse of the second-order necessary condition (3.21) is: if \(f\) satisfies \[ y^{T}\nabla f(x)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x)\,y\;>\;0 \] for all \(x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f\) and all \(y\neq0\), then \(f\) is quasiconvex. B&V §3.4.3 · L5189–5195 · (3.22)

Two differences from (3.21), and both are load-bearing:

  • strictness
    The conclusion is \(>0\), not \(\ge0\). Strict positive curvature in every critical direction is what powers the proof below.
  • y ≠ 0
    The quantifier excludes \(y=0\), which would otherwise make the hypothesis \(0>0\) and impossible to satisfy.
!

The gap. Necessary (3.21) uses \(\ge\); sufficient (3.22) uses \(>\). So the two are not an "if and only if". A function may satisfy (3.21) with equality somewhere and still be quasiconvex — or not; the second-order test simply does not decide those cases. This is unlike the convex second-order condition (3.11), where \(\nabla^{2}f\succeq0\) is exactly equivalent to convexity.

where the gap bitesf(t) = t³ again

\(f(t)=t^{3}\) is quasiconvex, and it satisfies (3.21): the only point with \(f'(t)=0\) is \(t=0\), and there \(f''(0)=0\ge0\). But it fails (3.22), which would need \(f''(0)>0\). So (3.22) is genuinely only sufficient — a function can be quasiconvex without passing it. In the other direction, \(f(t)=-t^{2}\) has \(f'(0)=0\) and \(f''(0)=-2<0\), so it fails (3.21) and is therefore not quasiconvex; the necessary condition does have teeth.

§6proof sketch

Why both conditions hold

Both proofs use the same opening move, the one from §3.4.2: quasiconvexity is decided one line at a time, so restrict \(f\) to an arbitrary line and it suffices to prove the statements for \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\).

Claim  (3.21) is necessarya strict local max would disconnect a sublevel set
\[ \text{restrict to a line: it suffices to prove } f'(c)=0\Rightarrow f''(c)\ge0 \text{ for } f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} \]
reduction. \(f\) is quasiconvex iff its restriction to every line is; the condition \(y^T\nabla f=0,\ y^T\nabla^2 f y\ge0\) is exactly the one-dimensional condition read along the direction \(y\).
\[ f'(c)=0,\ f''(c)<0 \;\Longrightarrow\; c \text{ is a strict local maximum} \]
contradiction hypothesis. suppose the condition fails at \(c\). zero slope with strictly negative curvature is the second-derivative test for a strict local max.
\[ \{x\mid f(x)\le f(c)-\epsilon\}\ \text{is disconnected for small } \epsilon>0 \]
near \(c\) the function dips below \(f(c)\) on both sides, so this sublevel set contains points to the left and to the right of \(c\) but not \(c\) itself.
\[ \text{disconnected} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{nonconvex} \;\Longrightarrow\; f \text{ not quasiconvex} \]
on \(\mathbb{R}\) a convex set is an interval, hence connected. contradiction, so no such \(c\) exists.
Claim  (3.22) is sufficientf′ can cross zero at most once
\[ f'(c)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; f''(c)>0 \quad\text{(the one-dimensional form of (3.22))} \]
the hypothesis. at every zero of \(f'\), the derivative \(f'\) is strictly increasing.
\[ \Longrightarrow\ f' \text{ crosses } 0 \text{ at most once, and only upward} \]
a function that is strictly increasing at each of its zeros cannot return to zero: after one upward crossing it stays positive.
\[ \Longrightarrow\ f \text{ is nonincreasing, then nondecreasing} \]
either \(f'\) never vanishes (\(f\) monotone) or it changes sign once from \(-\) to \(+\) (\(f\) valley-shaped).
\[ \Longrightarrow\ \text{every sublevel set is an interval} \;\Longrightarrow\; f \text{ quasiconvex} \]
monotone and valley-shaped are two of the three shapes in the §3.4.2 trichotomy on \(\mathbb{R}\); both have interval sublevel sets.
!

Notice where the strictness is spent. With only \(f''(c)\ge0\) the derivative could touch zero, flatten, and dip back below — the argument that \(f'\) crosses zero once would collapse. That is the entire reason (3.22) needs \(>\) while (3.21) only gives \(\ge\).

§7expert lens · diagnose

What to have at your fingertips

01The first-order condition, stated exactly

(3.20): \(f(y)\le f(x)\Rightarrow\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)\le0\). Against (3.2): convexity bounds the value, quasiconvexity only constrains the direction of non-increase.

02The geometry in one sentence

For \(\nabla f(x)\ne0\), \(\nabla f(x)\) is the outward normal of a supporting hyperplane to the sublevel set \(\{y\mid f(y)\le f(x)\}\) at \(x\).

03The big contrast

\(\nabla f(x)=0\) gives a global minimizer for convex \(f\), but not for quasiconvex \(f\) — witness \(x^{3}\) at \(0\). Stationary points are unreliable for quasiconvex minimization.

04Second-order necessary

(3.21): \(y^{T}\nabla f(x)=0\Rightarrow y^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x)y\ge0\). On \(\mathbb{R}\): \(f'=0\Rightarrow f''\ge0\). On \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\): \(\nabla^{2}f\) is PSD on \(\nabla f(x)^{\perp}\), hence at most one negative eigenvalue.

05Second-order sufficient

(3.22): the same with strict \(>\) for \(y\ne0\). Necessary uses \(\ge\), sufficient uses \(>\) — the two are not identical, and neither is an "iff".

06The traps

Don't write (3.20) as the convex bound. The hyperplane is supporting, with \(\nabla f\) outward. It is "at most one negative eigenvalue", not zero. And keep the \(\ge\) vs \(>\) gap straight.
