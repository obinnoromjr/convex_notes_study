§0where we are

The fifth convexity-preserving operation, and a surprising one. §3.2.3 warned that the pointwise minimum of a family generally is not convex. Yet a special form of minimization does yield a convex function: partially minimizing a jointly convex function over a convex set. Telling these two apart is the whole point of the section.

§1the minimization rule

If \(f\) is convex in \((x,y)\) jointly and \(C\) is a convex nonempty set, then

\[ g(x)=\inf_{y\in C} f(x,y) \tag{3.16}\] is convex in \(x\), provided \(g(x)>-\infty\) for all \(x\). Its domain is the projection onto \(x\): \(\operatorname{dom}g=\{x\mid (x,y)\in\operatorname{dom}f\text{ for some }y\in C\}\).B&V §3.2.5 · p. 87, eq. 3.16

Three load-bearing hypotheses, and each is doing real work:

  • H1
    \(f\) is jointly convex in \((x,y)\) — not merely convex in \(x\) for each fixed \(y\). This is the hypothesis the family version lacks.
  • H2
    \(C\) is a convex set, so that a convex combination of feasible \(y\)'s stays feasible.
  • H3
    \(g(x)>-\infty\) for all \(x\) — the infimum must not run off to \(-\infty\), or "convex" is vacuous.
+

Drop joint convexity and the rule fails. Convexity of \(f\) in \(x\) for each \(y\) separately is not enough — that is exactly the family case (§2), which is generally non-convex.

§2the contrast with §3.2.3 — min of a family

The section lives entirely in the gap between two operations that look alike but behave oppositely:

operationformresult
§3.2.3\(\min\{f_1,\dots,f_m\}\) of separate convex functionsgenerally not convex
§3.2.5\(\inf_{y\in C} f(x,y)\), \(f\) jointly convexconvex

Why do they diverge? A family \(\{f_i\}\) is like \(f(x,i)\) with \(i\) a discrete index. But a discrete index set is not convex, and \(f(x,i)\) is not jointly convex in \((x,i)\). Joint convexity together with a convex minimization set \(C\) is precisely what the family version is missing — supply both, and convexity is restored.

§3proof by ε-Jensen

Take \(x_1,x_2\in\operatorname{dom}g\), \(\theta\in[0,1]\), and any \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(\varepsilon\)-suboptimal \(y_1,y_2\in C\) with \(f(x_i,y_i)\le g(x_i)+\varepsilon\).

g satisfies Jensenthe ε absorbs an unattained inf
\[ g(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2)\ \le\ f\big(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2,\ \theta y_1+(1-\theta)y_2\big) \]
feasibility. \(\theta y_1+(1-\theta)y_2\in C\) since \(C\) is convex, so it is one admissible \(y\) — the inf is \(\le\) its value.
\[ \le\ \theta f(x_1,y_1)+(1-\theta)f(x_2,y_2) \]
joint convexity. the one step that uses \(f\) convex in \((x,y)\) together — not separately.
\[ \le\ \theta g(x_1)+(1-\theta)g(x_2)+\varepsilon \]
ε-suboptimality. replace each \(f(x_i,y_i)\) by \(g(x_i)+\varepsilon\).
\[ g(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2)\ \le\ \theta g(x_1)+(1-\theta)g(x_2) \]
ε → 0. the chain holds for every \(\varepsilon>0\); let \(\varepsilon\to0\) to get Jensen for \(g\). ∎
!

The \(\varepsilon\) is not decoration — it handles a possibly-unattained infimum. If the inf were always achieved you could pick exact minimizers; the \(\varepsilon\)-suboptimal \(y_i\) exist regardless, and letting \(\varepsilon\to0\) closes the gap.

§4the epigraph view — projection

The cleanest proof is one line of set geometry. The epigraph of \(g\) is

\[ \operatorname{epi}g=\big\{(x,t)\ \big|\ (x,y,t)\in\operatorname{epi}f\text{ for some }y\in C\big\}, \] the projection onto \((x,t)\) of \(\operatorname{epi}f\) restricted to \(y\in C\) — that is, of \(\operatorname{epi}f\cap(\mathbf R^n\times C\times\mathbf R)\), a convex set.B&V §3.2.5 · p. 88

A projection is a linear image, and linear images of convex sets are convex — so \(\operatorname{epi}g\) is convex, hence \(g\) is convex. This is the mirror of the §3.2.3 picture:

operationepigraph action
sup (§3.2.3)\(\operatorname{epi}\big(\sup_i f_i\big)=\bigcap_i \operatorname{epi}f_i\) — intersection
inf (§3.2.5)\(\operatorname{epi}g=\) projection of \(\operatorname{epi}f\) (drop the minimized \(y\)) — projection
+

The pair to memorize: \(\ \sup \leftrightarrow \text{intersection of epigraphs};\quad \inf \leftrightarrow \text{projection of the epigraph}.\) Both operations stay inside the convex sets — the family min fails because a discrete index gives a union, not a projection.

§5examples

Three that you actually reach for — each an instance of \(g(x)=\inf_{y\in C}f(x,y)\):

Ex 3.16 · distance to a setconvex if S convex

\(\operatorname{dist}(x,S)=\inf_{y\in S}\lVert x-y\rVert\). Since \(\lVert x-y\rVert\) is jointly convex in \((x,y)\), partial minimization over a convex \(S\) gives a convex distance function. To a nonconvex \(S\) it is generally not convex — the "\(S\) convex" hypothesis is exactly \(H2\).

Ex 3.15 · Schur complementA − BC⁻¹Bᵀ ⪰ 0, free

Let \(f(x,y)=x^{\mathsf T}Ax+2x^{\mathsf T}By+y^{\mathsf T}Cy\). It is convex in \((x,y)\) iff \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\B^{\mathsf T}&C\end{smallmatrix}\right]\succeq0\). Minimizing out \(y\) gives \(g(x)=\inf_y f=x^{\mathsf T}\!\big(A-BC^{\dagger}B^{\mathsf T}\big)x\), which is convex — so the Schur complement \(A-BC^{\dagger}B^{\mathsf T}\succeq0\) falls out for free.

Ex 3.17 · inf under a linear equalityconvex

For \(h\) convex, \(g(x)=\inf\{h(y)\mid Ay=x\}\) is convex (assuming \(g>-\infty\)). Take \(f(x,y)=h(y)\) on the affine set \(\{Ay=x\}\) and \(+\infty\) elsewhere; \(f\) is jointly convex, and the rule applies.

§6expert lens — what to keep straight (diagnose)

D1Partial min of a JOINTLY convex f is convex

Joint convexity in \((x,y)\), \(C\) convex, \(g>-\infty\). Drop joint convexity — convex in \(x\) for each \(y\) only — and it fails.

D2NOT the min of a family

§3.2.3's \(\min\{f_i\}\) is generally non-convex. Joint convexity + a convex minimization set \(C\) is exactly the difference (a discrete index is neither).

D3Epigraph meaning is PROJECTION

Drop the minimized coordinates: \(\operatorname{epi}g\) is the projection of \(\operatorname{epi}f\cap(\mathbf R^n\times C\times\mathbf R)\). Pair it: sup ↔ intersection, inf ↔ projection.

D4The ε handles an unattained inf

\(\varepsilon\)-suboptimal \(y_1,y_2\); \(\theta y_1+(1-\theta)y_2\in C\); joint convexity; then \(\varepsilon\to0\). No need for the inf to be achieved.

D5Consequences fall out

Schur complement \(A-BC^{\dagger}B^{\mathsf T}\succeq0\) for free; \(\operatorname{dist}(x,S)\) convex for convex \(S\); \(\inf\{h(y)\mid Ay=x\}\) convex.

Main points for quick review

Rule (3.16)

\(f\) jointly convex in \((x,y)\), \(C\) convex nonempty, \(g(x)=\inf_{y\in C}f(x,y)\) convex (provided \(g>-\infty\)); \(\operatorname{dom}g\) = projection onto \(x\).

Contrast

Partial min of a jointly convex \(f\), not min of a family (§3.2.3). Joint convexity + convex \(C\) is the difference.

ε-Jensen

\(\varepsilon\)-suboptimal \(y_1,y_2\in C\); \(\theta y_1+(1-\theta)y_2\in C\); \(f\) jointly convex; \(\varepsilon\to0\).

Epigraph

\(\operatorname{epi}g\) = projection onto \((x,t)\) of \(\operatorname{epi}f\cap(\mathbf R^n\times C\times\mathbf R)\) — convex. sup ↔ intersection; inf ↔ projection.

Examples

\(\operatorname{dist}(x,S)\) convex for convex \(S\); Schur \(A-BC^{\dagger}B^{\mathsf T}\succeq0\); \(\inf\{h(y)\mid Ay=x\}\) convex.
