Log-concave and log-convex functions

Take the logarithm first, then ask about curvature. The class you get is the natural one for probability densities and for \(\det X\) — and its chord inequality compares the value at the average with a geometric mean, not an arithmetic one.

§0where we are

A multiplicative cousin of convexity

§3.4 loosened convexity in one direction: quasiconvexity kept the sublevel sets convex and threw away everything else. §3.5 goes a different way. It keeps a genuine curvature condition, but applies it to \(\log f\) rather than to \(f\) — a class with a multiplicative flavour, since a condition on \(\log f\) becomes a condition on products and powers of \(f\).

This is the natural convexity notion for objects that are already multiplicative: probability densities, which multiply under independence; and \(\det X\), which multiplies under products. Log-concavity is the property those objects actually have — the multivariate normal density is not concave, but its logarithm is. This subsection gives the definition, its geometric-mean form, where the class sits relative to convex and quasiconvex, and the standard examples.

§1definition

Curvature after taking the log

A function \(f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}\) is logarithmically concave (or log-concave) if \(f(x)>0\) for all \(x\in\mathop{\bf dom} f\) and \(\log f\) is concave. It is logarithmically convex (log-convex) if \(\log f\) is convex. B&V §3.5.1 · L5337–5342

Two clauses, both load-bearing. The positivity clause is what makes \(\log f\) defined at all; the curvature clause is applied to \(\log f\), never to \(f\). It is worth saying out loud what the definition does not say: log-concave does not mean \(f\) is concave, and it does not mean \(\log f\) is positive.

  • domain
    \(\mathop{\bf dom}(\log f)=\mathop{\bf dom} f\), so log-concavity of \(f\) requires \(\mathop{\bf dom} f\) to be convex (as concavity of \(\log f\) does).
  • reciprocal
    \(f\) is log-convex if and only if \(1/f\) is log-concave, since \(\log(1/f)=-\log f\) and negating flips convex to concave. The two notions are exact mirror images through \(f\mapsto 1/f\).
  • both
    Nothing forbids a function from being both: that happens exactly when \(\log f\) is affine, i.e. \(f(x)=e^{a^{T}x+b}\).
!

The convention for zeros. It is convenient to allow \(f\) to take the value \(0\), and to use the extended-value convention \(\log f(x)=-\infty\) there. In that case \(f\) is log-concave if and only if the extended-value function \(\log f\) is concave — the \(-\infty\) values are absorbed by the extended-value definition of concavity, and \(\{x\mid f(x)>0\}\) plays the role of the domain. This is what lets a density that is \(0\) outside a set still count as log-concave.

!

The trap. Log-concavity is a condition on \(\log f\), not on \(f\). \(f\) itself need not be concave, and typically is not — the Gaussian bump \(e^{-x^{2}/2}\) is log-concave but has convex tails. Conversely \(f\) concave (and nonnegative) does give log-concave, but that is a theorem (§3), not the definition.

§2chord form

The geometric-mean characterization

A function \(f\) with convex domain and \(f(x)>0\) for all \(x\in\mathop{\bf dom} f\) is log-concave if and only if for all \(x,y\in\mathop{\bf dom} f\) and \(0\le\theta\le 1\), \[ f\bigl(\theta x+(1-\theta)y\bigr)\;\ge\; f(x)^{\theta}\,f(y)^{1-\theta}. \] B&V §3.5.1 · L5343–5350

This is the definition with the logarithm cleared away. It is worth reading it beside the ordinary concavity inequality, because the two differ in exactly one place — the kind of mean taken on the right:

\[ \underbrace{f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\;\ge\;\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)}_{\text{concave: arithmetic mean}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\;\ge\;f(x)^{\theta}f(y)^{1-\theta}}_{\text{log-concave: geometric mean}} \]

In words: the value at the weighted average of two points is at least the weighted geometric mean of the values at the points. Since the geometric mean never exceeds the arithmetic mean, the log-concave requirement is the weaker of the two — which is the seed of the implication in §3 that a nonnegative concave function is log-concave.

Claim  \(\log f\) concave \(\iff\) the geometric-mean inequalityJensen, then exponentiate
\[ \log f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\;\ge\;\theta\log f(x)+(1-\theta)\log f(y) \]
start this is exactly the statement that \(\log f\) is concave, written out for two points (Jensen's inequality for the pair \(x,y\)).
\[ e^{\,\log f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)}\;\ge\;e^{\,\theta\log f(x)+(1-\theta)\log f(y)} \]
the move apply \(\exp\), which is strictly increasing, so the inequality survives intact — and, being a bijection onto \(\mathbf{R}_{++}\), the step is reversible. That reversibility is what makes this an iff, not just an implication.
\[ f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\;\ge\;f(x)^{\theta}f(y)^{1-\theta} \]
simplify \(e^{\log f}=f\) on the left; on the right \(e^{\theta\log f(x)}=f(x)^{\theta}\) and likewise for \(y\). A sum of logs upstairs has become a product of powers downstairs.
◆

Why this form is the useful one. Written with the logarithm, log-concavity looks like an extra composition to check. Written as a geometric mean it is a statement you can test on any \(f\) directly, without ever forming \(\log f\) — and it is the form that generalizes: the same product-of-powers pattern is what makes log-concavity survive multiplication of functions and integration (§3.5.2).

§3hierarchy

Where the class sits — and which arrows are one-way

Log-convexity and log-concavity are closed under positive scaling and composition with an affine function. A log-convex function is convex; a nonnegative concave function is log-concave. A log-convex function is quasiconvex, and a log-concave function is quasiconcave, since the logarithm is monotone increasing. B&V §3.5.1 · L5351–5354

Four separate statements, each with a one-line reason. Three of them are compositions from §3.2.4; the fourth is the observation that a monotone transformation cannot change a sublevel set.

The four implicationscomposition + monotonicity
\[ f\ \text{log-convex}\;\Longrightarrow\; f\ \text{convex} \]
why write \(f=e^{\log f}=h\circ g\) with \(h=\exp\), \(g=\log f\). Here \(h\) is convex and nondecreasing and \(g\) is convex, so the composition is convex. Both properties of \(h\) are needed.
\[ f\ \text{concave},\ f\ge 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; f\ \text{log-concave} \]
why \(\log f=h\circ g\) with \(h=\log\), \(g=f\). Here \(h\) is concave and nondecreasing and \(g\) is concave, so the composition is concave. Nonnegativity is what puts \(f\) in the domain of \(\log\).
\[ \{x\mid f(x)\le t\}=\{x\mid \log f(x)\le \log t\}\quad (t>0) \]
why \(\log\) is strictly increasing, so \(f\) and \(\log f\) have the same sublevel sets (up to relabelling the level). Log-convex \(\Rightarrow\) \(\log f\) convex \(\Rightarrow\) its sublevel sets are convex \(\Rightarrow\) \(f\) quasiconvex.
\[ \{x\mid f(x)\ge t\}=\{x\mid \log f(x)\ge \log t\}\quad (t>0) \]
why the same argument on superlevel sets: log-concave \(\Rightarrow\) \(\log f\) concave \(\Rightarrow\) convex superlevel sets \(\Rightarrow\) \(f\) quasiconcave. Note the direction — log-concave gives quasiconcave, never quasiconvex.

Together with the closure properties (positive scaling, affine precomposition — both immediate from \(\log(\alpha f)=\log\alpha+\log f\) and \(\log f(Ax+b)=(\log f)(Ax+b)\)), this places log-convex strictly inside convex, and log-concave strictly inside quasiconcave:

\[ \text{log-convex}\;\subset\;\text{convex}\;\subset\;\text{quasiconvex}, \qquad \text{nonneg.\ concave}\;\subset\;\text{log-concave}\;\subset\;\text{quasiconcave}. \]
!

The arrows are one-way. Convex does not imply log-convex. Take \(f(x)=x^{2}\) on \(\mathbf{R}_{++}\): it is convex, but \(\log f(x)=2\log x\) is concave, so \(x^{2}\) is log-concave, not log-convex. So a function can be convex and log-concave at the same time — the two conditions sit on different objects and constrain each other only in the directions listed above.

§4example 3.39

The standard examples

B&V's list. Each is settled by writing \(\log f\) and reading its curvature off directly — that is the whole method.

affine functionlog-concave

\(f(x)=a^{T}x+b\) is log-concave on \(\{x\mid a^{T}x+b>0\}\). Reason: \(f\) is concave (affine, hence concave) and positive there, so §3 applies. L5357–5359

powers on \(\mathbf{R}_{++}\)the sign of \(a\) decides

\(f(x)=x^{a}\) on \(\mathbf{R}_{++}\) is log-convex for \(a\le 0\) and log-concave for \(a\ge 0\). Reason: \(\log f(x)=a\log x\), and \(\log x\) is concave; multiplying a concave function by \(a\ge0\) keeps it concave, by \(a\le 0\) flips it to convex. L5360–5362

Note the contrast with ordinary convexity of \(x^{a}\), which is governed by \(a\le 0\) or \(a\ge 1\) (§3.1.5). The log-version has a single clean cut at \(a=0\).

exponentialboth

\(f(x)=e^{ax}\) is log-convex and log-concave, because \(\log f(x)=ax\) is affine — and an affine function is both convex and concave. This is the boundary case of §1: \(f\) is both exactly when \(\log f\) is affine. L5363–5365

Gaussian cumulative distribution \(\Phi\)log-concave

\[ \Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du \] is log-concave. (Established in exercise 3.54; it also follows from the general fact of §3.5.2 that the integral of a log-concave function against a log-concave one stays log-concave.) L5366–5370

gamma function \(\Gamma\)log-convex for \(x\ge 1\)

\[ \Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{x-1}e^{-u}\,du \] is log-convex for \(x\ge 1\) (exercise 3.52). Being log-convex, it is therefore also convex there, by §3. L5371–5374

determinant on \(\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}\)log-concave

\(f(X)=\det X\) is log-concave on \(\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}\): \(\log\det X\) is concave (§3.1.5). This is the marquee matrix example, and the reason \(-\log\det X\) is the standard barrier for semidefinite programming. L5375–5376

determinant over tracelog-concave

\[ f(X)=\frac{\det X}{\operatorname{\bf tr} X} \] is log-concave on \(\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}\) (exercise 3.49). A ratio, so its log is a difference of two logs — and it is not the naive sum-of-concaves argument that settles it. L5377–5378

§5example 3.40

Log-concave densities — a recognition gallery

Many common probability densities are log-concave. The pattern to internalise is that you never check the density itself; you write down \(\log f\) and look at its shape.

multivariate normal\(\log f\) is a concave quadratic

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\det\Sigma}}\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-\bar{x})^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\bar{x})}, \qquad \Sigma\in\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}. \] Taking logs, \(\log f(x)=\text{const}-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\bar{x})^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\bar{x})\), a quadratic with \(-\Sigma^{-1}\preceq 0\), hence concave. So \(f\) is log-concave — although \(f\) itself is certainly not concave. L5385–5393

exponential distribution on \(\mathbf{R}^{n}_{+}\)\(\log f\) is affine

\[ f(x)=\Bigl(\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\Bigr)e^{-\lambda^{T}x},\qquad \lambda_i>0,\ x\succeq 0. \] \(\log f(x)=\sum_i\log\lambda_i-\lambda^{T}x\) is affine on \(\mathbf{R}^{n}_{+}\), so \(f\) is log-concave — indeed log-convex too on that domain, by the affine-log criterion. The density is \(0\) off \(\mathbf{R}^{n}_{+}\), which the \(-\infty\) convention of §1 handles, since \(\mathbf{R}^{n}_{+}\) is convex. L5394–5401

uniform distribution on a convex set\(\log f\) constant on \(C\), \(-\infty\) off it

\[ f(x)=\begin{cases}1/\alpha & x\in C\\ 0 & x\notin C\end{cases},\qquad \alpha=\operatorname{vol}(C). \] Then \(\log f=-\log\alpha\) on \(C\) and \(-\infty\) elsewhere. Because \(C\) is convex, this extended-value function is concave, so \(f\) is log-concave. This example is precisely why the \(-\infty\) convention was introduced. L5402–5410

Wishart distribution on \(\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}\)log-concave when \(p>n\)

\(X\in\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}\) is Wishart if its density has the form \[ f(X)=a\,(\det X)^{(p-n-1)/2}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\bf tr}(\Sigma^{-1}X)}, \] with \(a>0\) a normalising constant. Taking logs, \[ \log f(X)=\log a+\tfrac{p-n-1}{2}\log\det X-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\bf tr}(\Sigma^{-1}X). \] The last term is linear in \(X\); the middle term is a nonnegative multiple of the concave \(\log\det X\) provided \(p\ge n+1\). So \(\log f\) is concave and the Wishart density is log-concave when \(p>n\). L5411–5419

!

Where the hypothesis is used. In the Wishart case, \(p>n\) is exactly what makes the coefficient \((p-n-1)/2\) nonnegative. If it were negative, the middle term would be a negative multiple of a concave function — convex — and the argument would collapse. This is the same "check the sign of the multiplier" step as in the \(x^{a}\) example.

§6expert lens · diagnose

What mastery of this subsection looks like

01The definition, both clauses

Log-concave \(=\) \(f>0\) on \(\mathop{\bf dom} f\) and \(\log f\) concave; log-convex \(=\) \(\log f\) convex. And \(f\) log-convex \(\iff\) \(1/f\) log-concave.

02The geometric-mean form and its derivation

Log-concave \(\iff f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\ge f(x)^{\theta}f(y)^{1-\theta}\). Get it by Jensen on \(\log f\), then exponentiate; the step is reversible because \(\exp\) is a strictly increasing bijection.

03The hierarchy, with the direction of each arrow

Log-convex \(\Rightarrow\) convex; nonnegative concave \(\Rightarrow\) log-concave; log-convex \(\Rightarrow\) quasiconvex; log-concave \(\Rightarrow\) quasiconcave. None of these reverses — convex \(\not\Rightarrow\) log-convex, witnessed by \(x^{2}\), which is convex but log-concave.

04The marquee examples, on sight

\(e^{ax}\) both; \(x^{a}\) log-convex for \(a\le0\) and log-concave for \(a\ge0\); affine log-concave where positive; \(\Phi\) log-concave, \(\Gamma\) log-convex on \(x\ge1\); \(\det X\) and \(\det X/\operatorname{\bf tr}X\) log-concave on \(\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}\); the standard densities log-concave.

05The traps

Log-concave needs \(f>0\) (or the \(-\infty\) convention), not \(f\) concave. Log-concave gives quasiconcave, not quasiconvex. For \(x^{a}\) it is the sign of \(a\) that decides, not the \(a\le0\)-or-\(a\ge1\) rule for ordinary convexity.

Log-concave and log-convex functions

definition

Log-concave \(=\) \(f>0\) and \(\log f\) concave; log-convex \(=\) \(\log f\) convex. \(f\) log-convex \(\iff\) \(1/f\) log-concave.

geometric-mean form

Log-concave \(\iff f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\ge f(x)^{\theta}f(y)^{1-\theta}\) — Jensen on \(\log f\), then exponentiate.

hierarchy (one-way)

Log-convex \(\Rightarrow\) convex; nonneg. concave \(\Rightarrow\) log-concave; log-convex \(\Rightarrow\) quasiconvex; log-concave \(\Rightarrow\) quasiconcave. Convex \(\not\Rightarrow\) log-convex: \(x^{2}\).

examples

\(e^{ax}\) both; \(x^{a}\) log-convex \((a\le0)\) / log-concave \((a\ge0)\); affine; \(\Phi\) log-concave, \(\Gamma\) log-convex \((x\ge1)\); \(\det X\) log-concave on \(\mathbf{S}^{n}_{++}\).

densities

Normal, exponential, uniform-on-convex, Wishart with \(p>n\) — all log-concave. Recognise them by the shape of \(\log f\).

traps

Needs \(f>0\) (or \(\log f=-\infty\) at zeros), not \(f\) concave. Log-concave \(\Rightarrow\) quasiconcave, not quasiconvex. For \(x^{a}\) the sign of \(a\) decides.
