Operations that preserve quasiconvexity

Three constructions — nonnegative weighted max/sup, composition with a nondecreasing map, and minimization over a convex set — and the one operation conspicuously missing from the list: addition.

§0where we are

The quasiconvex calculus

§3.4.1–§3.4.3 defined quasiconvexity and gave its calculus conditions — the modified Jensen inequality (3.19), the first-order condition (3.20), the second-order conditions (3.21)–(3.22). This subsection is the quasiconvex calculus: the analog of §3.2 for convexity, listing which operations keep a function quasiconvex. There are three of them — nonnegative weighted maximum and supremum, composition, and minimization — and the requirements are notably weaker than in the convex case. One entry from the convex list is missing, and its absence is the sharpest thing to learn here: sums are dropped.

§1operation 1 · max and sup

Nonnegative weighted maximum and supremum

A nonnegative weighted maximum of quasiconvex functions, \[ f \;=\; \max\{\,w_1 f_1,\ \dots,\ w_m f_m\,\},\qquad w_i\ge 0,\quad f_i\ \text{quasiconvex}, \] is quasiconvex. The property extends to the general pointwise supremum \[ f(x)\;=\;\sup_{y\in C}\ \bigl(\,w(y)\,g(x,y)\,\bigr), \] where \(w(y)\ge 0\) and \(g(\cdot,y)\) is quasiconvex in \(x\) for each \(y\in C\). B&V §3.4.4 · L5218–5237

The proof is the sublevel-set argument, and it is worth memorizing in the form below because every other statement in this subsection is a variant of it.

Claim — the \(\alpha\)-sublevel set of a sup is an intersection of sublevel setsso it is convex
\[ f(x)\le\alpha \iff w(y)\,g(x,y)\le\alpha \quad\text{for all } y\in C \]
why a supremum is \(\le\alpha\) exactly when every member of the family is \(\le\alpha\). This single equivalence turns one condition on \(f\) into a family of conditions, one per \(y\).
\[ \{x\mid f(x)\le\alpha\}\;=\;\bigcap_{y\in C}\ \{x\mid w(y)g(x,y)\le\alpha\} \]
why "for all \(y\)" is set intersection. The \(\alpha\)-sublevel set of \(f\) is the intersection of the \(\alpha\)-sublevel sets of the individual functions \(w(y)g(\cdot,y)\).
\[ \text{each } \{x\mid w(y)g(x,y)\le\alpha\} \text{ is convex} \]
why \(g(\cdot,y)\) is quasiconvex and \(w(y)\ge 0\), so \(w(y)g(\cdot,y)\) is quasiconvex too (scaling by a nonnegative constant just relabels which \(\alpha\) you are looking at), and quasiconvex functions have convex sublevel sets.
\[ \text{an intersection of convex sets is convex}\ \Rightarrow\ f \text{ quasiconvex} \]
why §2.3.1. Every sublevel set of \(f\) is convex, which is the definition of quasiconvexity (§3.4.1). Note that the index set \(C\) may be infinite — intersection does not care.
!

Compare the convex version (§3.2.3): there, the pointwise supremum of convex functions is convex because epigraphs intersect, \(\operatorname{epi} f=\bigcap_y \operatorname{epi} g(\cdot,y)\). Here the same move is made one level down, on sublevel sets. Same argument, different object — and that is why the hypotheses can be weakened from convex to quasiconvex.

!

The weights must be nonnegative. A negative \(w_i\) turns \(\{w_if_i\le\alpha\}\) into a superlevel set of \(f_i\), which quasiconvexity says nothing about. And it is a maximum, not a sum — see §5.

§2example 3.37

Maximum generalized eigenvalue

Example 3.37 Generalized eigenvalues. The maximum generalized eigenvalue of a symmetric-matrix pair \((X,Y)\), with \(Y\succ 0\), is defined as \[ \lambda_{\max}(X,Y)\;=\;\sup_{u\neq 0}\ \frac{u^{T}Xu}{u^{T}Yu}\;=\;\sup\{\lambda\mid \det(\lambda Y-X)=0\}. \] This function is quasiconvex on \(\mathbf{S}^n\times\mathbf{S}^n_{++}\). B&V §3.4.4 · L5239–5261 · example 3.37

The verification is §1 applied verbatim, and the family is indexed by the direction \(u\):

  • each member
    Fix \(u\neq0\). Both \(u^TXu\) and \(u^TYu\) are linear functions of the pair \((X,Y)\), so \((X,Y)\mapsto (u^TXu)/(u^TYu)\) is linear-fractional in \((X,Y)\), with denominator positive on the domain \(Y\succ0\). Linear-fractional functions are quasilinear (§3.4.1) — every sublevel set, and every superlevel set, is a halfspace.
  • the sup
    \(\lambda_{\max}\) is the pointwise supremum of that family over \(u\neq0\), with weights \(w(u)\equiv1\). By §1 it is quasiconvex.
  • the domain
    \(Y\succ 0\) is exactly what makes \(u^TYu>0\) for every \(u\neq0\); without it the ratio is undefined or changes sign and the linear-fractional structure is lost. Quasiconvexity is asserted jointly in \((X,Y)\) on \(\mathbf{S}^n\times\mathbf{S}^n_{++}\), not just in \(X\).

The second expression, \(\sup\{\lambda\mid\det(\lambda Y-X)=0\}\), says the same thing algebraically: \(\lambda\) is a generalized eigenvalue of the pair exactly when \(\lambda Y-X\) is singular, and the largest such \(\lambda\) is the largest value of the Rayleigh-type ratio. It is also the largest ordinary eigenvalue of \(Y^{-1}X\).

!

This is a clean case where a supremum of quasilinear functions is quasiconvex but not convex. Contrast the special case \(Y=I\): then \(u^TXu\) is linear in \(X\), and a pointwise sup of linear functions is convex — \(\lambda_{\max}(X)\) really is a convex function of \(X\) (§3.2.3). It is the dependence on \(Y\) that costs convexity and leaves only quasiconvexity.

the diagonal case, by hand\(\lambda_{\max}=\max_i x_{ii}/y_{ii}\)

Take \(X=\mathrm{diag}(x_1,x_2)\) and \(Y=\mathrm{diag}(y_1,y_2)\succ0\). Then \(\det(\lambda Y-X)=(\lambda y_1-x_1)(\lambda y_2-x_2)\), whose roots are \(x_1/y_1\) and \(x_2/y_2\), so \(\lambda_{\max}(X,Y)=\max\{x_1/y_1,\ x_2/y_2\}\) — literally a maximum of two linear-fractional functions of \((x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2)\), which is §1 with \(m=2\). Its \(\alpha\)-sublevel set is \(\{x_1\le\alpha y_1\}\cap\{x_2\le\alpha y_2\}\), an intersection of two halfspaces. The Rayleigh form gives the same answer: \((u^TXu)/(u^TYu)=(u_1^2x_1+u_2^2x_2)/(u_1^2y_1+u_2^2y_2)\) is a weighted average of \(x_1/y_1\) and \(x_2/y_2\) with weights \(u_1^2y_1\) and \(u_2^2y_2\), and an average is largest when all the weight sits on the larger ratio.

!

Dual remark, worth carrying: \(\lambda_{\min}(X,Y)=\inf_{u\ne0}(u^TXu)/(u^TYu)\) is an infimum of the same quasilinear family, hence quasiconcave on \(\mathbf{S}^n\times\mathbf{S}^n_{++}\). Minimizing \(\lambda_{\max}\) and maximizing \(\lambda_{\min}\) are therefore both quasiconvex problems — the reason generalized-eigenvalue minimization is solvable by bisection (§4.2.5).

§3operation 2 · composition

Composition

If \(g:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\) is quasiconvex and \(h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is nondecreasing, then \(f=h\circ g\) is quasiconvex. B&V §3.4.4 · L5264–5267

Read the hypothesis on \(h\) carefully: monotone, nothing more. It need not be convex, need not be differentiable, need not even be continuous.

Claim — \(\{x\mid h(g(x))\le\alpha\}\) is a sublevel set of \(g\)so it is convex
\[ \{t\in\mathbb{R}\mid h(t)\le\alpha\}\ \text{is an interval unbounded below} \]
why \(h\) is nondecreasing: if \(h(t)\le\alpha\) and \(s\le t\) then \(h(s)\le h(t)\le\alpha\). So the set is \((-\infty,\beta]\) or \((-\infty,\beta)\) with \(\beta=\sup\{t\mid h(t)\le\alpha\}\) (possibly empty, possibly all of \(\mathbb{R}\)).
\[ \{x\mid f(x)\le\alpha\}=\{x\mid g(x)\le\beta\}\ \ \text{or}\ \ \{x\mid g(x)<\beta\} \]
why composing the two: \(f(x)\le\alpha\) iff \(g(x)\) lands in that interval. Both a sublevel set and a strict sublevel set of a quasiconvex \(g\) are convex.
!

The contrast with the convex composition rule (§3.2.4) is the point. There you need \(g\) convex and \(h\) convex and \(h\) nondecreasing. Here you need \(g\) quasiconvex and \(h\) merely nondecreasing. Convexity of \(h\) is used, in the convex rule, to bound values; quasiconvexity only ever asks about sublevel sets, and monotonicity alone is enough to pull one back to another.

worked — \(f(x)=\arctan\|x\|_2\)\(h\) nondecreasing but not convex

Take \(g(x)=\|x\|_2\) (convex, hence quasiconvex) and \(h(t)=\arctan t\), which is nondecreasing on \(\mathbb{R}\) but concave for \(t\ge0\) and therefore not convex. The composition rule gives \(f=h\circ g\) quasiconvex, and the sublevel sets confirm it directly: for \(0\le\alpha<\pi/2\),

\[ \{x\mid \arctan\|x\|_2\le\alpha\}=\{x\mid \|x\|_2\le\tan\alpha\}, \]

a Euclidean ball — convex. But \(f\) is not convex, and a two-point witness settles it: with \(x=0\) and \(y=(4,0)\),

\[ f\!\left(\tfrac{x+y}{2}\right)=\arctan 2\approx 1.1071\ \;>\;\ \tfrac{f(x)+f(y)}{2}=\tfrac{0+\arctan 4}{2}\approx 0.6629. \]

Had the convex rule been applied it would have failed at the hypothesis "\(h\) convex" and delivered nothing. The quasiconvex rule is strictly stronger here because it asks for less.

Composition with an affine or linear-fractional map also preserves quasiconvexity. If \(f\) is quasiconvex then \(f(Ax+b)\) is quasiconvex, and \[ \tilde f(x)=f\!\left(\frac{Ax+b}{c^{T}x+d}\right) \] is quasiconvex on \(\ \{x\mid c^{T}x+d>0,\ (Ax+b)/(c^{T}x+d)\in\operatorname{\mathbf{dom}} f\}\). B&V §3.4.4 · L5268–5271

Both follow from the sublevel-set picture again: \(\{x\mid \tilde f(x)\le\alpha\}\) is the inverse image, under an affine or linear-fractional map, of the convex set \(\{z\mid f(z)\le\alpha\}\) — and inverse images of convex sets under those maps are convex (§2.3.2, §2.3.3). Note the domain restriction \(c^Tx+d>0\) is not cosmetic: it is what makes the linear-fractional map convexity-preserving on that piece.

§4operation 3 · minimization

Minimization

If \(f(x,y)\) is quasiconvex jointly in \((x,y)\) and \(C\) is a convex set, then the function \[ g(x)\;=\;\inf_{y\in C}\ f(x,y) \] is quasiconvex. B&V §3.4.4 · L5273–5279
Claim — \(\{x\mid g(x)\le\alpha\}\) is convexthe \(\varepsilon\)-argument, because an inf need not be attained
\[ g(x_1)\le\alpha,\quad g(x_2)\le\alpha,\quad \theta\in[0,1],\quad \varepsilon>0 \]
why take two points of the \(\alpha\)-sublevel set of \(g\) and a convex combination; the goal is \(g(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2)\le\alpha\).
\[ \exists\, y_1,y_2\in C:\quad f(x_i,y_i)\le\alpha+\varepsilon \]
why definition of infimum. The \(\varepsilon\) is load-bearing: \(g(x_i)\le\alpha\) does not give a \(y_i\) with \(f(x_i,y_i)\le\alpha\), because the infimum may not be attained. Everything after this is done at level \(\alpha+\varepsilon\).
\[ f\bigl(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2,\ \theta y_1+(1-\theta)y_2\bigr)\ \le\ \alpha+\varepsilon \]
why the points \((x_1,y_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2)\) both lie in the \((\alpha+\varepsilon)\)-sublevel set of \(f\), which is convex by joint quasiconvexity — so the whole segment between them lies in it too.
\[ \theta y_1+(1-\theta)y_2\in C\ \Rightarrow\ g\bigl(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2\bigr)\le\alpha+\varepsilon \]
why \(C\) convex, so that combination is an admissible \(y\); the infimum over \(C\) is at most the value at this particular one. This is where convexity of \(C\) is used, and the only place.
\[ \varepsilon>0 \text{ arbitrary}\ \Rightarrow\ g\bigl(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2\bigr)\le\alpha \]
why letting \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\). The sublevel set is convex for every \(\alpha\), so \(g\) is quasiconvex.
!

Joint quasiconvexity in \((x,y)\) is essential — quasiconvex in \(x\) for each fixed \(y\), and in \(y\) for each fixed \(x\), is not enough. The proof needs the segment from \((x_1,y_1)\) to \((x_2,y_2)\), which moves in both arguments at once; separate quasiconvexity says nothing about such a segment. Exactly the same warning attaches to the convex version, §3.2.5.

worked — distance to a convex setthe archetype of the construction

Let \(C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\) be convex and put \(f(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2\). As a function of the pair \((x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\) this is a norm composed with the linear map \((x,y)\mapsto x-y\), so it is convex jointly, hence quasiconvex jointly. The rule then gives

\[ \operatorname{dist}(x,C)=\inf_{y\in C}\|x-y\|_2 \quad\text{quasiconvex}, \]

with \(\{x\mid \operatorname{dist}(x,C)\le\alpha\}=C+\alpha B\), the \(\alpha\)-neighbourhood of \(C\) — visibly convex. Two things to notice. First, the hypothesis really is used jointly: had we only known that \(\|x-y\|\) is quasiconvex in \(x\) for each \(y\), the argument would not run. Second, the infimum is attained here when \(C\) is closed, but the proof never needed that — which is exactly why the \(\varepsilon\) appears.

§5the key contrast

Sums do not preserve quasiconvexity

B&V's list is max/sup, composition, minimization — conspicuously not addition. Nonnegative weighted sums head the convex list (§3.2.1); they are absent here, and not by oversight. Quasiconvexity is not preserved under sums.

!

Structurally, the reason is the same sublevel-set identity that made §1 work. For a maximum, \(\{\max_i w_if_i\le\alpha\}=\bigcap_i\{w_if_i\le\alpha\}\) — the condition decomposes across the family. For a sum there is no such decomposition: knowing \(f(x)+g(x)\le\alpha\) tells you nothing about \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) separately, so the sublevel set of \(f+g\) is not built out of the sublevel sets of \(f\) and \(g\), and their convexity buys nothing.

counterexample — \(x^3\) and \(-x\)both quasiconvex, sum is not
  • \(f(x)=x^3\)
    Monotone (increasing) on \(\mathbb{R}\), and every monotone function on \(\mathbb{R}\) is quasiconvex — its sublevel sets \(\{x\mid x^3\le\alpha\}=(-\infty,\alpha^{1/3}]\) are intervals.
  • \(g(x)=-x\)
    Affine, hence quasilinear: both its sublevel sets and superlevel sets are intervals.
  • \(f+g=x^3-x\)
    Not quasiconvex.

Two independent certificates of failure:

\[ (x^3-x)'=3x^2-1=0 \ \text{ at } \ x=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3},\qquad (x^3-x)''\big|_{x=-1/\sqrt3}=6\left(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right)=-\tfrac{6}{\sqrt3}\approx-3.464<0. \]

So at \(x=-1/\sqrt3\) the derivative vanishes and the second derivative is strictly negative — a strict local maximum. That violates the second-order necessary condition (3.21) of §3.4.3, which on \(\mathbb{R}\) reads \(f'(x)=0\Rightarrow f''(x)\ge0\). Directly, in terms of sublevel sets,

\[ \{x\mid x^3-x\le 0\}=\{x\mid x(x-1)(x+1)\le0\}=(-\infty,-1]\ \cup\ [0,1], \]

which is not an interval, hence not convex — quasiconvexity fails at \(\alpha=0\).

!

Practical consequence: when a model needs an objective that is a sum of terms, quasiconvexity of the pieces is worthless and you must establish convexity (or reformulate). Quasiconvexity is a good property to have for a single objective you intend to minimize by bisection on \(\alpha\) (§4.2.5), and a poor one to try to build compositionally out of sums.

§6expert lens · diagnose

What to have on instant recall

01Max / sup

\(\max\{w_if_i\}\) with \(w_i\ge0\), and \(\sup_{y\in C}w(y)g(x,y)\) with \(w(y)\ge0\), are quasiconvex. The reason in one line: the \(\alpha\)-sublevel set is the intersection of the members' \(\alpha\)-sublevel sets, each convex.

02Generalized eigenvalue (Ex 3.37)

\(\lambda_{\max}(X,Y)=\sup_{u\ne0}(u^TXu)/(u^TYu)=\sup\{\lambda\mid\det(\lambda Y-X)=0\}\) is quasiconvex on \(\mathbf{S}^n\times\mathbf{S}^n_{++}\): each ratio is linear-fractional in \((X,Y)\), and a sup of quasiconvex functions is quasiconvex.

03Composition

\(h\circ g\) is quasiconvex if \(g\) is quasiconvex and \(h\) is nondecreasing — \(h\) need not be convex. Affine and linear-fractional precompositions preserve quasiconvexity, the latter on \(\{c^Tx+d>0\}\).

04Minimization

\(\inf_{y\in C}f(x,y)\) is quasiconvex if \(f\) is jointly quasiconvex and \(C\) is convex. The proof runs at level \(\alpha+\varepsilon\) because the infimum need not be attained.

05The big trap

Sums do not preserve quasiconvexity: \(x^3\) and \(-x\) are quasiconvex, \(x^3-x\) is not. Sharpest contrast with the convex calculus (§3.2.1), where nonnegative weighted sums are the first entry on the list.

06The two weakened hypotheses

Where the convex rules demand convexity, these demand less: \(h\) nondecreasing suffices (not convex). But one hypothesis is not weakened — minimization still needs joint quasiconvexity, not separate.

summary — main points for quick review

Operations that preserve quasiconvexity

max / sup

\(\max\{w_if_i\}\) (\(w_i\ge0\)) and \(\sup_{y}w(y)g(x,y)\) are quasiconvex — the \(\alpha\)-sublevel set is the intersection of convex sublevel sets.

generalized eigenvalue (Ex 3.37)

\(\lambda_{\max}(X,Y)=\sup_{u\ne0}(u^TXu)/(u^TYu)\) quasiconvex on \(\mathbf{S}^n\times\mathbf{S}^n_{++}\); each ratio linear-fractional in \((X,Y)\), sup of quasiconvex.

composition

\(h\circ g\) quasiconvex if \(g\) quasiconvex and \(h\) nondecreasing (not necessarily convex); affine and linear-fractional precompositions preserve quasiconvexity.

minimization

\(\inf_{y\in C}f(x,y)\) quasiconvex if \(f\) jointly quasiconvex and \(C\) convex.

contrast

Quasiconvexity is not preserved under sums: \(x^3\) and \(-x\) are quasiconvex, \(x^3-x\) is not — unlike convexity.
